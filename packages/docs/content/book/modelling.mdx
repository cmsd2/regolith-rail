## What you will learn

- To describe an operation as a model: the decisions to make, the objective that
  judges them, and the constraints they must respect.
- To see a policy as a rule that turns what a decision-maker observes into what it
  does, and to see why a sensible-sounding rule can still fail.
- To work out, with a little algebra, why balancing stock between two stations delivers
  too little, and to confirm it by running the simulator.

## The problem

Operations research studies systems that run by rules. A warehouse reorders stock, a
hospital schedules theatres, a railway decides what each train carries. In each, a
decision is made again and again, and the quality of the operation depends less on any
single decision than on the rule that makes them all.

Building a model is the first step. A model leaves out almost everything about the real
system and keeps what the decision depends on. Good models are small enough to reason
about and faithful enough that their conclusions survive contact with the real thing
(Taha, 2017; Eiselt and Sandblom, 2022).

This chapter builds a model of the smallest interesting freight line: a supply station
that produces a resource, a demand station that consumes it, and one vehicle that runs
back and forth between them.

## The model

### Decisions, objectives and constraints

Every model in this book has the same three parts.

- **Decisions.** What the operator controls. Here, how much the vehicle loads or unloads
  each time it stops.
- **Objective.** How decisions are judged. Here, first, how much demand goes unmet at the
  demand station, and second, how much production stalls because the supply station is
  full.
- **Constraints.** What any decision must respect. Stock never goes below zero or above
  the station's storage, cargo never exceeds the vehicle's capacity, and every unit that
  leaves one place arrives in another.

The last constraint, conservation, is worth writing down. Over any stretch of time, a
station's stock changes by what was produced, consumed, loaded and unloaded there:

$$
s(t_2) = s(t_1) + \text{produced} - \text{consumed} - \text{loaded} + \text{unloaded}.
$$

<Check ref="test:conserves resources and keeps stock and cargo within bounds at every event" />

### A policy is a rule

A decision made once can be optimised directly. A decision made at every stop, for ever,
needs a rule. A policy maps what the vehicle observes at a stop, its cargo and the stock
at the stations, to how much to load or unload. The simulator calls a policy at every
stop, and the results measure the policy, not any one choice.

### The line in numbers

In the `two-station` scenario the stations are 72,000 distance units apart and the
vehicle travels at 5 units a second, so each leg takes 14,400 seconds, four hours.

<Check ref="maxima:leg-time" />

A stop takes 600 seconds plus 60 seconds for each unit moved. The slowest round trip,
moving a full load of 30 units at both ends, takes $28/3$ hours. Even then the vehicle
makes $18/7$ trips a sol (a day of 24 hours), and could carry

$$
30 \times \frac{18}{7} = \frac{540}{7} \approx 77.1 \text{ units a sol},
$$

<Check ref="maxima:capacity-bound" />

more than twice the 36 units a sol the demand station consumes. Capacity is not the
problem.

### Balancing

A natural rule is to balance: at each stop, move stock so that the stations hold similar
amounts. With two stations holding $m$ and $d$, the average is $(m + d)/2$, so a stop at
the fuller station loads

$$
m - \frac{m + d}{2} = \frac{m - d}{2},
$$

<Check ref="maxima:half-difference" />

half the difference, however much room the vehicle has.

Suppose the line settles into a steady cycle, where each trip delivers what the demand
station uses during one round trip. Call the load $L$ and the round trip $T$. The dome
uses $36\,T$ units in a trip lasting $T$ sols, and the trip lasts the travel time plus two
stops moving $L$ units each. Solving the two together gives

$$
T = \frac{600\,000}{19} \text{ s} \approx 8.77 \text{ h}, \qquad L = \frac{250}{19} \approx 13.16 \text{ units}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:steady-load" />

Balancing loads half the difference, so to load $L$ the supply station must hold $2L$
more than the demand station:

$$
2L = \frac{500}{19} \approx 26.3 \text{ units},
$$

<Check ref="maxima:required-gap" />

nearly 88% of a 30-unit station. To move enough, the supply end must sit close to full
and the demand end close to empty. Any disturbance then pushes one end over the edge: the
supply station fills and production stalls, or the demand station empties and demand
goes unmet.

## In the simulator

The `two-station` starter is this line. The supply station produces 45 units a sol, the
demand station consumes 36 units a sol, varying by up to 20% either way, and the run
lasts 10 sols. The baseline policy balances stock.

<Scenario starter="two-station" />

Run the baseline and open **Metrics**. On every one of seeds 1 to 20, demand goes unmet
at the demand station while production stalls at the supply station.

<Check ref="test:on two-station the naive baseline leaves demand unmet while the mine's production stalls, on each of 20 seeds" />

## Case study: half capacity

The model says the fault is in the rule, not the line. A rule that treats the stations
by their role instead of their stock takes everything it can at the supply end and leaves
everything at the demand end. The vehicle then carries full loads when there is stock to
carry, and the gap between the stations stops mattering.

```lua runnable fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual { Mine = "supply", Dome = "demand" },
  target = { supply = ops.drain {}, demand = ops.fill {} },
}
```

This policy meets all demand on every one of seeds 1 to 20.

<Check ref="test:on two-station taking everything from the mine and leaving everything at the dome meets all demand on each of 20 seeds" />

Production still stalls under it. The supply station makes 9 units a sol more than the
demand station uses, so once the demand station is full the surplus has nowhere to go.
That is a fact about the line, not a failure of the policy.

<Check ref="maxima:surplus" />

Compare the two policies in **Batch** to see the difference over many seeds, and what the
role-based rule costs in [empty distance](/docs/metrics#empty-distance-share): the vehicle
now always returns empty.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Stops take longer with more cargo.** The simple model above treats the steady cycle
  as exact. In the simulator, demand varies, so loads and trip times vary from trip to
  trip around the steady values.
- **Stock is measured in thousandths of a unit.** Policies see and move amounts in
  milli-units, so 30 units is `30000`.
- **The balancing rule is an assumption.** The baseline implements balancing as the
  model describes it. How closely it matches any real system is recorded, with its
  evidence, in [game mechanics](/docs/game-mechanics#station-balancing).

## Exercises

1. At the top of its variability the demand station uses 20% more, 43.2 units a sol.
   Under balancing, what gap between the stations does a steady cycle need, and can a
   30-unit station provide it?

<Answer>

The steady load becomes $750/47 \approx 15.96$ units, so the gap is $1500/47 \approx 31.91$
units. That is more than a 30-unit station can hold, so at peak demand balancing cannot
keep up however the stocks start.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-peak-demand" />

</Answer>

2. A faster vehicle travels at 10 units a second. What load and gap does the steady cycle
   need under balancing?

<Answer>

Each leg takes two hours. The steady load is $130/19 \approx 6.84$ units, so the gap is
about 13.68 units. Faster trips need smaller loads, so balancing copes better, but the
stations still have to hold very different amounts.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-faster-train" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

In the rail game, trains appear to balance stock across the stations on a line, which is
the rule this chapter models. A mine at one end of a long line and a dome at the other is
exactly the two-station case: the dome runs short and the mine's extractors stop, even
with a train that could carry far more.
