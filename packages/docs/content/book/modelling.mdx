## What you will learn

- To write a decision as a model: the variable you choose, the parameters you are given, the constraints
  that limit the choice and the objective that judges it.
- To describe a decision made again and again as a system with a state equation, and a policy as the
  rule that picks each action from what it observes.
- To check whether any policy can work before comparing policies, and to find the steady state a
  policy settles into.
- To predict from that steady state what a simulation will show, and how the prediction moves when the
  line changes.

## The problem

Operations research studies decisions through models. A model describes an operation in simplified form.
It includes the quantities a decision depends on and omits everything else. The omissions cost some
accuracy, and in return the model can be solved and studied (Eiselt and Sandblom, 2022, pp. 4–5). The best
solution to a model is the best plan for the operation only when the model represents the operation well
(Taha, 2017, p. 34).

This chapter models the smallest freight line with something to decide. A mine at one end of the line
produces metal into its station. A habitat dome at the other end uses metal from its station. One train
runs back and forth between the two stations, and at every stop it decides how much to load or unload.
The decision comes up at every stop for as long as the line runs, so the question is which rule should
make it.

## The model

### One decision

Take a single stop first. The train is at the mine with nothing aboard, and has to choose how much metal
to load. That amount, $a$, is the **decision variable**: the number the operator controls. The rest are
**parameters**, numbers that are given (Eiselt and Sandblom, 2022, pp. 5–6). The mine's station has
$m$ units in stock. The train can carry at most $\kappa = 30$. The dome's station has a capacity of
$\bar x = 30$ and will have $d$ units in stock when the train gets there. The **constraints** say which choices are possible, and the
**objective** says which possible choice is best. Moving as much as possible gives the model

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{maximise}\quad & a \\
\text{subject to}\quad & a \le m, \quad a \le \kappa, \quad a \le \bar x - d, \quad a \ge 0,
\end{aligned}
$$

whose solution is $a^* = \min(m, \kappa, \bar x - d)$. A choice that meets every constraint is
**feasible**, and a feasible choice with the best objective is **optimal** (Taha, 2017, pp. 33–34). With
20 units in stock at the mine ($m = 20$) and 12 at the dome when the train arrives ($d = 12$), the train
loads 18. At that solution the dome's room constraint holds as an equation, $a = \bar x - d$, and the
other two have **slack**: the mine has more in stock and the train could carry more. A constraint that
holds as an equation at the optimal solution is a **binding constraint** (Taha, 2017, p. 119).

<Check ref="maxima:one-stop" />

The operation wants the dome never to run short and the mine never to stop for lack of room. "Move as much
as possible" stands in for those aims. It is a **proxy objective**, one that can be measured and
optimised, and it says nothing about what happens after this stop (Eiselt and Sandblom, 2022, p. 5).

### Repeated decisions

The train reaches the dome once every round trip. Let $x_k$ be the dome's stock when the train arrives
for the $k$-th time, $a_k$ what the train unloads, and $\xi_k$ what the dome would use before the next
arrival. Demand that finds the dome's station empty is lost. Writing $(y)^+$ for $\max(y, 0)$, the stock
at the next arrival and the demand that goes unmet are

$$
x_{k+1} = (x_k + a_k - \xi_k)^+, \qquad w_k = (\xi_k - x_k - a_k)^+,
$$

with the constraints that $a_k$ is at most what the train carries and $x_k + a_k \le \bar x$. This is a
**state equation**. The stock is the state of the system, the amount unloaded is the action, and demand
is a disturbance the operator does not control. Hernández-Lerma et al. (2023, p. 4) write a production
and inventory system in this form. Nothing appears or disappears: the stock that was there plus what the train
delivered equals what is left plus what the dome used, $x_{k+1} + \xi_k - w_k = x_k + a_k$.

<Check ref="maxima:state-equation" />

Choosing $a_k$ once is the problem of the previous section. Choosing it at every stop needs a
**policy**, a rule $\pi$ that maps what the train observes to an action: the stock at each station and
the cargo aboard. A policy that acts on the observed state is a feedback, or closed-loop, policy
(Hernández-Lerma et al., 2023, p. 5). The objective becomes a property of the policy. This chapter uses
$J(\pi) = \sum_k w_k$, the demand left unmet over a run, and reports the production the mine loses for
lack of room beside it.

### The line

The two stations are 72,000 distance units apart and the train travels at 5 a second, so a leg takes
4 hours. A stop takes 10 minutes, plus a minute for every unit moved. The rest of the chapter, and its
exercises, use these parameters unless they say otherwise:

| Parameter | Symbol | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time for one leg | $\tau$ | 4 hours |
| Time for a stop, before moving anything | $t_0$ | 10 minutes, $1/6$ hour |
| Extra time for a stop, for each unit moved | $t_1$ | 1 minute, $1/60$ hour |
| The train's capacity | $\kappa$ | 30 units |
| Each station's capacity | $\bar x$ | 30 units |
| The mine's production | | 45 units a day |
| The dome's use, on average | $u$ | 36 units a day, $3/2$ an hour |

<Check ref="maxima:leg-time" />

### Feasibility

A study checks that the operation can work at all before it asks what works best (Eiselt and Sandblom,
2022, p. 7). The slowest round trip moves a full load at both stops, and even then the train carries

$$
T_{\max} = 2\tau + 2(t_0 + \kappa t_1) = \tfrac{28}{3}\ \text{hours}, \qquad
\frac{24\,\kappa}{T_{\max}} = \frac{540}{7} \approx 77.1\ \text{units a day},
$$

more than twice the 36 a day the dome uses.

<Check ref="maxima:capacity-bound" />

The mine makes 9 units a day more than the dome uses. No rule can move that surplus anywhere useful, so
once the dome's station is full the mine stops for lack of room, on average 9 units a day.

<Check ref="maxima:surplus" />

### Two rules

The policies in this book are written with the `ops` library. At every stop the policy looks at each
*site*, one station and one resource it stores. It may first classify the site: `ops.roles.manual` gives
each station a role by name, such as `"supply"` for the mine and `"demand"` for the dome. It then sets a
target, the stock level the site should have when the train leaves, and the train loads or unloads the
difference, as far as its cargo and the room available allow. `ops.drain {}` targets nothing, so
the train takes all it can. `ops.fill {}` targets the station's capacity, so the train leaves all it can.
`ops.balance {}` targets the average stock of the stations that store the resource. The
[ops reference](/docs/ops) lists every block, and a policy can be plain Lua when the blocks do not fit.

The first rule uses the roles: drain the mine and fill the dome. The second is balancing, which needs no
roles. It is the policy a game might adopt for every line a player builds, and the workbench's baseline,
`balance-stock`:

```lua
return ops.policy { target = ops.balance {} }
```

With $m$ units at the mine and $d$ at the dome, the average is $(m + d)/2$, so a stop at the mine loads

$$
m - \frac{m + d}{2} = \frac{m - d}{2},
$$

half the difference between the stations, however much room the train has.

<Check ref="maxima:half-difference" />

### What the dome needs

Suppose the line settles into a **steady state**, where every round trip repeats the one before. Each
trip then delivers what the dome uses in one round trip: a load $\bar a = uT$, where the round trip $T$
includes two stops that each move $\bar a$ units. Solving

$$
\bar a = uT, \qquad T = 2\tau + 2(t_0 + t_1 \bar a)
\qquad\text{gives}\qquad
\bar a = \frac{2u(\tau + t_0)}{1 - 2u t_1}.
$$

For this line $\bar a = 250/19 \approx 13.16$ units and $T = 500/57 \approx 8.77$ hours. The
drain-and-fill rule reaches this state: it leaves the dome's station full at every stop, so each trip
replaces what the dome used since the last, and the dome never runs short.

<Check ref="maxima:needed-load" />

### Balancing in steady state

Balancing cannot fill the dome's station: it unloads only up to the average stock of the two stations,
which reaches 30 only when both are full. Suppose instead that the line settles with the mine's station
full at every mine stop, the dome's station running out of stock before each delivery, and the train
unloading its whole load at the dome. At the mine stop the dome has $d$ units in stock and the mine has
$\bar x$, so the train loads $a = (\bar x - d)/2$. That $d$ is what remains of the
last load after the stop at the dome and the leg back, which take $t_0 + t_1 a + \tau$ hours. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= \frac{\bar x - d}{2}, \qquad d = a - u(\tau + t_0 + t_1 a), \\
a &= \frac{\bar x + u(\tau + t_0)}{3 - u t_1} = \frac{1450}{119} \approx 12.18\ \text{units},
\end{aligned}
$$

with a round trip of $1040/119 \approx 8.74$ hours.

<Check ref="maxima:balancing-load" />

The three assumptions hold at this solution. The dome's stock at the mine stop lasts less than the time
until the train arrives. The mine refills faster than the train takes metal away. At the dome, half
the difference between the stations is more than the train carries.

<Check ref="maxima:balancing-assumptions" />

Each trip carries less than the dome uses in a round trip, so the dome runs out before every delivery.
The train delivers $24a/T = 435/13 \approx 33.46$ units a day, and

$$
24u - \frac{24a}{T} = 36 - \frac{435}{13} = \frac{33}{13} \approx 2.54\ \text{units a day}
$$

go unmet, while the mine loses $150/13 \approx 11.54$ units a day for lack of room.

<Check ref="maxima:balancing-shortfall" />

## In the simulator

The workbench's built-in scenarios are its starters. The `two-station` starter is this line, run for
10 days with the balancing baseline. A run draws its random numbers from a **seed**, a number that fixes
them, so the same seed always gives the same run. This chapter looks at one run at a time. Chapter 3 asks
how much the results would change with another seed.

<Scenario starter="two-station" />

Open the drain-and-fill rule, the library's suggested fix for this starter:

```lua runnable scenario=two-station fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual { Mine = "supply", Dome = "demand" },
  target = { supply = ops.drain {}, demand = ops.fill {} },
}
```

The Scenario tab shows the line as a scenario script, Lua that builds the stations and the train. The dome's consumer reads `rate = 36, variability = 20`. Change the 20 to 0, so that the dome
uses exactly 36 a day as the model assumes, and run. Click a stop marker at the mine on the timeline to see
in the stop inspector what the train loaded. The model predicts that loads settle at 13.16 units and the
dome never runs short.

Now put <PolicyLink policy="balance-stock">`balance-stock`</PolicyLink> in the Policy slot. The scenario
keeps its edit. Run again. The model predicts loads of 12.18 units and a dome that runs out on every trip.
To measure the rate at which demand goes unmet once the line has settled, set the scenario's
`duration = sols(20)` and compare the unmet demand in **Metrics** with a run of `sols(5)`.

<Predicted>

| Steady demand | Model | Simulator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drain and fill: units loaded a trip | 13.16 | 13.15 to 13.18 |
| Drain and fill: round trip, hours | 8.77 | 8.77 |
| Balancing: units loaded a trip | 12.18 | 12.18 |
| Balancing: round trip, hours | 8.74 | 8.74 |
| Balancing: unmet a day, days 5 to 20 | 2.54 | 2.53 |

</Predicted>

The simulator advances a minute at a time and moves stock in thousandths of a unit, which accounts for the
last digit. With steady demand nothing in the run is random, so every seed gives the same result.

Set the variability back to 20 and run both rules again on seed 1. Balancing's loads now vary from trip to
trip between 11 and 14 units, and 25.3 units go unmet over the 10 days. The drain-and-fill rule still meets
all of the dome's demand. How far a second run would differ from the first is the subject of chapter 3.

## Case study: half capacity

The train on this line could carry more than twice what the dome uses, yet balancing leaves the dome
short. Two requirements meet at the mine stop. Balancing loads half the difference between the stations,
so to load $\bar a$ the mine's stock must exceed the dome's by $2\bar a$. The dome must also have enough in
stock at that moment to last until the train reaches it, $u(\tau + t_0 + t_1 \bar a)$. With the mine's
station full, both fit only if each station's capacity is at least

$$
\bar x_{\min} = 2\bar a + u(\tau + t_0 + t_1 \bar a) = \frac{625}{19} \approx 32.89\ \text{units}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:smallest-station" />

Of those 32.89 units, $2\bar a \approx 26.32$ is the difference in stock balancing needs and $6.58$ is
what the dome uses while the train comes from the mine. The stations' capacity is 30. At $\bar x_{\min}$, balancing's
steady load from the model section equals $\bar a$. Below it, balancing settles at the smaller load and
the dome runs out every trip. The mine's station sits full and the dome's sits nearly empty, so the line
runs at both of its limits at once.

The threshold is a **sensitivity** result: it says how the conclusion moves when a parameter moves
(Eiselt and Sandblom, 2022, p. 7). The needed load $\bar a$ grows with the dome's demand $u$ and with the
leg time $\tau$, and $\bar x_{\min}$ grows with both. Shorter legs or larger stations bring balancing
back within its limit, and a busier dome takes it further out. The exercises work through
three of these.

The drain-and-fill rule leaves the dome full at every stop, so it needs no station capacity beyond the
load it delivers. Balancing needs no knowledge of which station supplies and which demands. On this line
it needs a station capacity of 32.89 units in exchange, and the stations have 30.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Minutes and thousandths.** The simulator advances in one-minute steps and moves stock in thousandths
  of a unit, so its loads settle at 12.18 where the model gives 12.185.
- **Decisions on arrival.** A policy decides when the train arrives, using the stock at both stations at
  that moment. The model assumes the same. A rule that saw older figures would settle elsewhere.
- **Demand in two-hour steps.** With variability on, the dome's rate is drawn afresh every two hours,
  uniformly within 20% either side of 36 a day.
- **The simulator keeps the same account as the state equation** at every event: stock and cargo stay
  between zero and capacity, and the totals of every resource balance.

  <Check ref="test:conserves resources and keeps stock and cargo within bounds at every event" />
- **The balancing rule is an assumption.** The baseline implements balancing as this chapter describes
  it. [Game mechanics](/docs/game-mechanics#station-balancing) records how closely that matches the game,
  with its evidence.

## Exercises

Each exercise uses the parameters in the table under [The line](#the-line), except where it says
otherwise.

1. Use the one-stop model. The train reaches the mine with nothing aboard and can carry 30 units. The
   mine's station has 25 units in stock. The dome's station has a capacity of 30 and will have 22 units in
   stock when the train arrives. How much does the train load, and which is the binding constraint?

<Answer>

$a^* = \min(m, \kappa, \bar x - d) = \min(25, 30, 30 - 22) = 8$ units. The binding constraint is the
dome's room, $a^* = \bar x - d$. The mine's stock and the train's capacity have slack.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-one-stop" />

</Answer>

2. The dome's use varies up to 20% either side of its average. At the top of that range it uses 43.2 units
   a day, $u = 1.8$ units an hour. Keep every other parameter from the table. Find:
   - the steady load $\bar a$ the dome needs, from [What the dome needs](#what-the-dome-needs);
   - the smallest station capacity $\bar x_{\min}$ at which balancing keeps up, from the case study;
   - balancing's steady load $a$ and round trip $T$ with the stations' capacity of 30, from
     [Balancing in steady state](#balancing-in-steady-state);
   - the demand left unmet a day, $24u - 24a/T$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar a &= \frac{2 \times 1.8 \times (4 + \tfrac16)}{1 - 2 \times 1.8 \times \tfrac{1}{60}} = \frac{750}{47} \approx 15.96, \\
\bar x_{\min} &= 2\bar a + 1.8\,(4 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac{1}{60}\bar a) \approx 31.91 + 7.98 = 39.89, \\
a &= \frac{30 + 1.8\,(4 + \tfrac16)}{3 - \tfrac{1.8}{60}} = \frac{37.5}{2.97} \approx 12.63, \qquad
T = 8 + 2\,(\tfrac16 + \tfrac{a}{60}) \approx 8.75\ \text{hours}, \\
24u - \frac{24a}{T} &\approx 43.2 - 34.62 = 8.58\ \text{units a day}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The dome needs almost 16 units a trip. Balancing would need a station capacity of about 40, and with 30 it
delivers about 12.6 a trip.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-peak-demand" />

</Answer>

3. Halve the line. In the scenario script, change `distances = 72000` to `distances = 36000`, so each leg
   takes $\tau = 2$ hours, and keep the dome's variability at 0. Every other parameter stays as in the
   table. Work out $\bar a$ and $\bar x_{\min}$ for the shorter line, and decide from the stations'
   capacity of 30 whether balancing keeps up. Then run `balance-stock` and compare.

<Answer>

$$
\bar a = \frac{2 \times 1.5 \times (2 + \tfrac16)}{1 - 2 \times 1.5 \times \tfrac{1}{60}} = \frac{130}{19} \approx 6.84,
\qquad
\bar x_{\min} = 2\bar a + 1.5\,(2 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac{1}{60}\bar a) = \frac{325}{19} \approx 17.11.
$$

A station capacity of 30 is more than 17.11, so balancing keeps up. The run meets all demand, with loads of
6.84 units a trip.

<Check refs="
  maxima:exercise-short-line
  test:on two-station with steady demand and the line halved, balancing meets all demand at 6.84 units a trip
" />

</Answer>

4. Raise both stations' capacity to 60 units. In the scenario script, change both `mars.small_station` to
   `mars.large_station`, which has a capacity of 60 for each resource, and keep the dome's variability at
   0. Everything else stays as in the table. Compare 60 with $\bar x_{\min}$ from the case study. Then run
   `balance-stock` for 5 days and for 10 days (`duration = sols(5)` and `sols(10)`) and compare the unmet
   demand in **Metrics**.

<Answer>

The line's parameters are unchanged, so $\bar x_{\min}$ is still 32.89, and 60 is above it: balancing can
keep up once the stations' stock levels have moved apart. Both runs show 4.84 units unmet, so all of it
goes unmet in the first five days, while the mine's stock rises and the dome's falls. By day 10 the loads
have come down to the needed 13.16 units.

" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

In the rail game, trains appear to balance stock across the stations on a line, the rule this chapter
models. A game has to work for every line a player builds, so a rule that needs no roles is a reasonable
default. A mine at one end of a long line and a dome at the other is where it costs most: the dome runs
short and the mine's extractors stop, although the train could carry far more.
