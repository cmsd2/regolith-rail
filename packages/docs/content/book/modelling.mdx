## What you will learn

- To describe an operation as a model: the decisions to make, the objective that
  judges them, and the constraints they must respect.
- To see a policy as a rule that turns what a decision-maker observes into what it
  does, and to see why a sensible-sounding rule can still fail.
- To check with a little algebra whether a simple rule can work on a line, and to see why
  a rule that ignores what each station does can deliver too little.

## The problem

Operations research studies systems that run by rules. A warehouse reorders stock, a
hospital schedules theatres, a railway decides what each train carries. In each, a
decision is made again and again, and the quality of the operation depends less on any
single decision than on the rule that makes them all.

Building a model is the first step. A model leaves out almost everything about the real
system and keeps what the decision depends on. Good models are small enough to reason
about and faithful enough that their conclusions survive contact with the real thing
(Taha, 2017; Eiselt and Sandblom, 2022).

This chapter builds a model of the smallest interesting freight line: a supply station
that produces a resource, a demand station that consumes it, and one vehicle that runs
back and forth between them.

## The model

### Decisions, objectives and constraints

Every model in this book has the same three parts.

- **Decisions.** What the operator controls. Here, how much the vehicle loads or unloads
  each time it stops.
- **Objective.** How decisions are judged. Here, first, how much demand goes unmet at the
  demand station, and second, how much production stalls because the supply station is
  full.
- **Constraints.** What any decision must respect. Stock never goes below zero or above
  the station's storage, cargo never exceeds the vehicle's capacity, and every unit that
  leaves one place arrives in another.

The last constraint, conservation, is worth writing down. Over any stretch of time, a
station's stock changes by what was produced, consumed, loaded and unloaded there:

$$
s(t_2) = s(t_1) + \text{produced} - \text{consumed} - \text{loaded} + \text{unloaded}.
$$

<Check ref="test:conserves resources and keeps stock and cargo within bounds at every event" />

### A policy is a rule

A decision made once can be optimised directly. A decision made at every stop, for ever,
needs a rule. A policy maps what the vehicle observes at a stop, its cargo and the stock
at the stations, to how much to load or unload. The simulator calls a policy at every
stop, and the results measure the policy, not any one choice.

### Writing a rule down

The policies in this book are written with the `ops` library, which builds a policy from
a few named parts. Every chapter uses them, so they are worth naming now. At each stop the
policy looks at each *site*, one station and one resource it stores, and decides in
stages:

- **Classify** gives the site a role: `supply`, where the resource is made; `demand`,
  where it is used; `relay`, where it only passes through; or `any`, when the policy does
  not say. `ops.roles.manual` lists the roles by station.
- **Target** says how much stock the site should hold when the train leaves. It is one
  block for every site, or a block for each role. `ops.drain {}` targets nothing, so the
  train takes all it can; `ops.fill {}` targets the site's capacity, so the train leaves
  all it can; `ops.balance {}` targets the mean stock across the stations that store the
  resource; and `ops.pass_through {}` names no target, so the site is left as it is.
- The difference between the target and what the site holds is what the train loads or
  unloads, as far as its cargo, its space and the station's room allow.

A policy that gives only a target treats every site alike. One that classifies first can
treat a supply site and a demand site differently, and that is the whole difference
between the two rules in this chapter. The [ops reference](/docs/ops) lists every block,
and a policy can always be plain Lua instead, for rules the blocks cannot express.

### The line

The line in this chapter joins two stations 72,000 distance units apart. At one end is a
mine, which produces metal at 45 units a day into its station. At the other is a habitat
dome, which uses 36 units a day from its station, sometimes up to 20% more or less. Each
station holds at most 30 units. One train runs back and forth between them. It carries up
to 30 units and travels at 5 units a second, so each leg takes 14,400 seconds, four hours.
A stop takes 10 minutes, plus a minute for each unit moved.

<Check ref="maxima:leg-time" />

In operations research terms, the mine's station is a supply point, the dome's station a
demand point, and the train a vehicle on a fixed route.

### The obvious rule: take everything, leave everything

Knowing which station makes metal and which uses it, the first rule most people think of
is to load everything at the mine and unload everything at the dome. Does the train have
the capacity for that? Each leg takes four hours, and a stop takes ten minutes plus a
minute for every unit moved, so even the slowest round trip, moving a full load of 30 at
both ends, takes $2 \times 4 + 2 \times (10 + 30)/60 = 28/3$ hours. In a day the train
makes $24 \div 28/3 = 18/7$ such trips, and could carry

$$
30 \times \frac{18}{7} = \frac{540}{7} \approx 77.1 \text{ units a day},
$$

<Check ref="maxima:capacity-bound" />

more than twice the 36 units a day the dome uses. Over that slowest trip the mine makes
17.5 units, which fits in the train, and the dome uses 14. Every trip brings more than
the dome used while the train was away.

<Check ref="maxima:take-everything" />

The rule has one limit it cannot avoid. The mine makes 9 units a day more than the dome
uses, so once the dome's station is full the surplus has nowhere to go and the mine
stalls. That is a fact about the line, not a failure of the rule.

<Check ref="maxima:surplus" />

### A rule for any line: balancing

The obvious rule depends on knowing which station supplies and which demands. A rule
meant for every line, whatever its stations do, cannot assume that. A natural catch-all
is to balance: at each stop, move stock so that the stations hold similar amounts. It
needs no roles and copes with any number of stations. It is the built-in `balance-stock`
policy, the baseline the workbench opens with, and in `ops` it is a single line:

```lua
return ops.policy { target = ops.balance {} }
```

With two stations holding $m$ and $d$, the average is $(m + d)/2$, so a stop at the
fuller station loads

$$
m - \frac{m + d}{2} = \frac{m - d}{2},
$$

<Check ref="maxima:half-difference" />

half the difference, however much room the train has. The case study below follows that
through.

## In the simulator

The workbench's built-in scenarios are its starters, and the `two-station` starter is
this line, run for 10 days. It opens with the balancing
baseline, the built-in `balance-stock` policy.

<Scenario starter="two-station" />

Try the obvious rule first. As an `ops` policy, the mine's station is a supply site that
is drained and the dome's station a demand site that is filled:

```lua runnable fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual { Mine = "supply", Dome = "demand" },
  target = { supply = ops.drain {}, demand = ops.fill {} },
}
```

It meets all of the dome's demand on every one of seeds 1 to 20. Once the dome's station
is full, the mine can still stall, as the surplus says it must.

<Check ref="test:on two-station taking everything from the mine and leaving everything at the dome meets all demand on each of 20 seeds" />

Now put the balancing baseline back in the run: click `balance-stock`, or use it from the
**Policies** list in the library. Run it and open **Metrics**. On every one of seeds 1 to
20, the dome goes short while the mine stalls, at the same time.

<Check ref="test:on two-station the balancing baseline leaves demand unmet while the mine's production stalls, on each of 20 seeds" />

Compare the two in **Batch** to see the difference over many seeds, and what taking
everything costs in [empty distance](/docs/metrics#empty-distance-share): the train
always returns empty.

## Case study: half capacity

Why does balancing fail on a line where the obvious rule succeeds? Suppose the line
settles into a steady cycle, where each trip delivers what the dome uses during one round
trip. Call the load $L$, in units, and the round trip $T$, in seconds. The load is what
the dome uses in a trip, at 36 units a day, and the trip is the eight hours of travel plus
two stops that each take 600 seconds and 60 more for every unit moved:

$$
L = \frac{36\,T}{86\,400}, \qquad T = 28\,800 + 2\,(600 + 60\,L).
$$

<Check ref="maxima:steady-equations" />

Solving the two together gives

$$
T = \frac{600\,000}{19} \text{ s} \approx 8.77 \text{ h}, \qquad L = \frac{250}{19} \approx 13.16 \text{ units}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:steady-load" />

Balancing loads half the difference, so to load $L$ the mine's station must hold $2L$ more
than the dome's:

$$
2L = \frac{500}{19} \approx 26.3 \text{ units},
$$

<Check ref="maxima:required-gap" />

nearly 88% of a 30-unit station. To move enough, the mine's station must sit close to full
and the dome's close to empty. Any disturbance then pushes one end over the edge: the
mine's station fills and production stalls, or the dome's empties and demand goes unmet.
The train has the capacity; the rule only ever uses about half of the gap it sees.

Balancing pays for its generality. It ignores what we know about this line, that the mine
makes metal and the dome uses it, and on a line this simple that knowledge is the whole
difference between meeting demand and not. Much of the rest of this book is about putting knowledge like that to work.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Demand varies.** The case study treats the steady cycle as exact. In the simulator
  the dome's use wanders by up to 20%, so loads and trip times vary from trip to trip
  around the steady values.
- **Stock is measured in thousandths of a unit.** Policies see and move amounts in
  milli-units, so 30 units is `30000`.
- **The balancing rule is an assumption.** The baseline implements balancing as the
  model describes it. How closely it matches any real system is recorded, with its
  evidence, in [game mechanics](/docs/game-mechanics#station-balancing).

## Exercises

1. At the top of its variability the demand station uses 20% more, 43.2 units a day.
   Under balancing, what gap between the stations does a steady cycle need, and can a
   30-unit station provide it?

<Answer>

The steady load becomes $750/47 \approx 15.96$ units, so the gap is $1500/47 \approx 31.91$
units. That is more than a 30-unit station can hold, so at peak demand balancing cannot
keep up however the stocks start.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-peak-demand" />

</Answer>

2. A faster vehicle travels at 10 units a second. What load and gap does the steady cycle
   need under balancing?

<Answer>

Each leg takes two hours. The steady load is $130/19 \approx 6.84$ units, so the gap is
about 13.68 units. Faster trips need smaller loads, so balancing copes better, but the
stations still have to hold very different amounts.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-faster-train" />

</Answer>

3. Open the starter's script and change both stations to `mars.large_station`, which
   holds 60 units. Does balancing keep up now?

<Answer>

Mostly. The steady cycle needs a gap of about 26 units between the stations, and a
60-unit station holds it with room to spare, so over seeds 1 to 20 unmet demand falls
from about 28 units to about 5. But the dome still goes short on every seed: balancing
still moves half the gap it sees, so the dome sits low whenever the mine sits high, and
any disturbance closes the difference.

<Check ref="test:on two-station with 60-unit stations, balancing leaves about 5 units unmet over seeds 1 to 20 instead of about 28, but the dome still goes short on every seed" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

In the rail game, trains appear to balance stock across the stations on a line, which is
the catch-all rule this chapter models. A game has to work for every line a player
builds, so a rule that needs no roles is a reasonable default. A mine at one end of a long
line and a dome at the other is where it costs most: the dome runs short and the mine's
extractors stop, even with a train that could carry far more.

<Evidence level="observed" />

</GameNote>
