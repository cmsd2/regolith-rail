## What you will learn

- To track inventory position, stock on hand plus stock on order less backorders, and to
  see why decisions must use it rather than stock on hand.
- To find the protection interval an order must cover when stock is reviewed
  periodically and replenished after a lead time.
- To set a base-stock level with the critical fractile, and to run the policy that
  orders up to it.

## The problem

Chapter 5 orders once, for a single period. Most stock is not like that. A shop keeps
selling, reviews its shelves on a schedule, and reorders from a supplier who takes time
to deliver. Each order has to last until the next one can arrive, and in the meantime
earlier orders are still on their way.

Two things make this harder than it looks. First, the decision must count what is
already coming, or the same shortage gets ordered again and again. Second, an order
placed now cannot help until the lead time has passed, and the next order cannot help
until a review period after that, so each order protects against demand over a longer
stretch than the time until it arrives (Axsäter, 2015, pp. 39–43; Snyder and Shen, 2019,
pp. 105–113).

## The model

### Inventory position

Stock on hand is what is on the shelf. Inventory position also counts what has been
ordered but not delivered, and subtracts demand that is waiting to be met:

$$
\text{position} = \text{on hand} + \text{on order} - \text{backorders}.
$$

With 5 on hand, 7 on order and 2 backordered, the position is 10.

<Check ref="maxima:inventory-position" />

Orders already placed will arrive whatever happens next, so the position is what the
station will hold once they do. Deciding from the position counts every order exactly
once.

### The protection interval

Suppose stock is reviewed every $R$ and an order arrives a lead time $L$ after it is
placed. An order placed at a review arrives after $L$. The next order, placed a review
later, arrives after $R + L$. Until then, only this order and those before it can meet
demand. The order must therefore cover demand over the protection interval $L + R$.

With arrivals of 4 a day, a lead time of 2 days and reviews every 6 hours, the protection
interval is $9/4$ days and demand over it is Poisson with mean 9.

<Check ref="maxima:protection-interval" />

### Base stock

A base-stock policy raises the inventory position to a fixed level $S$ at every review,
ordering $S$ minus the position. What happens over the protection interval is then the
newsvendor problem of chapter 5. Each extra unit of $S$ costs $h$ for holding when it turns out not to
be needed, and saves $b$ for a backorder when it is. The best level is the critical
fractile of demand $N$ over the protection interval:

$$
S^* = \min\left\{ S : P(N \le S) \ge \frac{b}{b + h} \right\}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:critical-fractile" />

With a holding cost of 1 and a backorder cost of 10, the fractile is $10/11$. Demand
with mean 9 is at most 12 with probability below that and at most 13 with probability
about 0.926, so $S^* = 13$.

<Check ref="maxima:base-stock-level" />

### Why stock on hand fails

A policy that orders up to $S$ from stock on hand ignores what is on the way. With a
lead time of 2 days and reviews every 6 hours, the first order arrives only after 8 more
reviews, and at each of them the same shortage is still on the shelf and gets ordered
again.

<Check ref="maxima:repeated-orders" />

## In the simulator

The `classic.reorder` template has one station, replenished after a lead time, whose
unmet demand is backordered. Open it and turn on **random** for Poisson demand. This
version matches the worked case: 4 a day, a lead time of 2 days, reviews every 6 hours,
and no fixed order cost.

<Scenario template="classic.reorder" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.reorder {
  random = true,
  demand = 4,
  lead_time = days(2),
  review_period = hours(6),
  order_cost = 0,
  duration = days(30),
}
```

The reference policy orders up to 13, using the inventory position that every `ops`
target works from. The template works out its expected cost tick by tick from Poisson
probabilities: 7.97 a day.

```lua runnable scenario=classic.reorder
return ops.policy {
  review = { target = ops.order_up_to { level = 13000 } },
}
```

<Check ref="test:the reorder template's base-stock reference for 4 a day, a 2-day lead time and 6-hour reviews orders up to 13 and expects 7.97 a day" />

Over 200 seeds, the simulated cost per day agrees with that value within its confidence
interval.

<Check ref="test:reaches the analytic expected cost of a base-stock policy with random demand" />

Now order up to the same level from stock on hand, in plain Lua:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.reorder
return {
  on_review = function(ctx)
    local short = 13000 - ctx.here.stock.Goods
    if short > 0 then
      ctx.order("Goods", short)
    end
  end,
}
```

On seeds 1 to 100 it costs more than five times as much a day. Open the stock chart to
see why: stock climbs far above 13 as repeated orders for the same shortage arrive one
after another.

<Check ref="test:on classic.reorder with random demand, ordering up to 13 from stock on hand costs more than five times as much a day as ordering up to 13 from the inventory position, on seeds 1 to 100" />

## Case study: double dispatch

The `two-trains` starter has two trains sharing a line from a mine to a factory and a
dome. It was built to show double dispatch: under the balancing baseline, both trains react
to the same low stock, so one station can be oversupplied while the other waits. It looks like the stock-on-hand mistake: each train
judges a shortage without counting what the other is already bringing.

<Scenario starter="two-trains" />

The first remedy is to stop balancing and treat stations by their role: take everything
from the mine, and fill the factory and the dome.

```lua runnable scenario=two-trains fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual {
    Mine = "supply",
    Factory = "demand",
    Dome = "demand",
  },
  target = { supply = ops.drain {}, demand = ops.fill {} },
}
```

The second remedy adds [ops.lookahead](/docs/ops/lookahead), which reserves cargo for
stations further along, so that the other train counts it in their inventory position:

```lua runnable scenario=two-trains
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual {
    Mine = "supply",
    Factory = "demand",
    Dome = "demand",
  },
  target = { supply = ops.drain {}, demand = ops.fill {} },
  plan = ops.lookahead {},
}
```

The measurements do not flatter the second remedy. On seeds 1 to 100, roles alone leave
less than a tenth of the baseline's unmet demand. Adding lookahead leaves more unmet
demand than roles alone, and more than three times the empty running.

<Check ref="test:on two-trains over seeds 1 to 100, roles leave less than a tenth of the balancing baseline's unmet demand, and adding lookahead leaves more unmet demand than roles alone with over three times the empty running" />

A likely reason shows in how lookahead decides. At the mine, a train loads only what the
stations ahead are short of *now*. The dome is four hours' travel away and uses 7 units in
that time, and the factory, 100 minutes away, uses almost 3 units. Loading for today's
shortfall at the far end of a trip is the same error as ordering without a protection
interval: the cargo arrives too small for the demand it has to meet.

<Check ref="maxima:two-trains-travel" />

Counting what is on the way is necessary, but not enough. It has to be combined with
enough stock, or cargo, to cover demand until the next delivery can arrive.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Periodic, not continuous, review.** Many textbook models watch stock continuously.
  The simulator reviews on a schedule, a minute into each period, so continuous review is
  approximated by reviewing often.
- **Backorders only for the reference result.** With lost sales and a lead time, a
  base-stock policy is no longer the best, and the best policy has no simple form
  (Axsäter, 2015, pp. 97–99). The template gives a reference result only with backorders.
- **Whole cost units, charged as they accrue.** Holding and backorder costs are charged
  each minute on what is held or owed, in whole cost units per unit per day.

## Exercises

1. Keep the worked case but review once a day. What is the protection interval's mean
   demand, and the base-stock level?

<Answer>

The interval is $2 + 1 = 3$ days, so mean demand is 12, and the level rises to 17. Less
frequent reviews need more stock.

<Check ref="maxima:longer-review" />

</Answer>

2. Backorders become twice as costly, 20 a unit a day. What is the new level?

<Answer>

The fractile rises to $20/21$ and the level to 14.

<Check ref="maxima:dearer-backorders" />

</Answer>

3. The supplier halves the lead time to 1 day. What is the level now?

<Answer>

The protection interval shrinks to $5/4$ days with mean demand 5, and the level falls to
8. A shorter lead time saves 5 units of stock.

<Check ref="maxima:shorter-lead-time" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

On a rail line, a train's visit is a review and the time for cargo to reach a station is
a lead time. A train that counts only a station's stock, and not what another train is
already bringing, makes the stock-on-hand mistake above. Stations far apart, or served by
few trains, have long protection intervals and need fuller stocks to avoid running dry
between visits.

</GameNote>
