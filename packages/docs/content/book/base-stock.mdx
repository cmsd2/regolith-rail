## What you will learn

- To define the inventory level and the inventory position, and to prove how the level after a lead
  time depends on the position now.
- To define a base-stock policy and the protection interval that each of its orders must cover.
- To prove that the optimal base-stock level is a critical-ratio quantile of demand, and to bound it by
  the quantiles over the lead time and over the protection interval.
- To compute the excess that results from ordering on the stock on hand alone.

## The problem

The newsvendor of chapter 5 orders once, for one period. A station that keeps selling orders again and
again, and each order takes time to arrive. Two questions follow. The first is what an order should be
based on, given that earlier orders are still on their way. The second is how much demand an order must
cover before a later order can help.

The answers are the inventory position and the protection interval (Axsäter, 2015, pp. 39–43; Snyder and
Shen, 2019, pp. 105–113). With them, the choice of a stock level for a station with many periods reduces
to the newsvendor problem of <Ref to="thm-critical-ratio" />.

## The model

### The Shop with a lead time

<Assumption id="asm-shop-lead-time" name="The Shop with a lead time">

The Shop of <Ref to="asm-shop" /> is changed as follows.

1. Demand is a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, as in <Ref to="def-poisson-process" />.
2. The Shop reviews its stock at intervals $R$ and may order any quantity at a review. An order has no
   fixed cost.
3. An order arrives a lead time $L$ after it is placed.
4. Demand that finds no stock is **backordered**: it waits, and is met when stock arrives.
5. Stock on hand costs $h$ per unit per unit time, and each backordered unit costs $p$ per unit time.

</Assumption>

The chapter's examples and exercises use the following values, with time in days.

| Parameter | Symbol | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand rate | $\lambda$ | 10 units a day |
| Review period | $R$ | 1 hour, $1/24$ day |
| Lead time | $L$ | 1 day |
| Cost of holding a unit | $h$ | 1 a day |
| Cost of a backordered unit | $p$ | 10 a day |

Without a fixed cost there is no reason to combine orders, and the question of chapter 4, how much to
order at a time, gives way to the question of how much stock to keep.

### Inventory position

<Definition id="def-inventory-position" name="Inventory level and inventory position">

The **inventory level** is the stock on hand less the backorders. The **inventory position** is the
inventory level plus the stock on order, that is, ordered and not yet arrived. Write $\mathit{IL}(t)$ and
$\mathit{IP}(t)$ for their values at time $t$.

</Definition>

Stock on hand and backorders are never both positive, since arriving stock first meets the backorders. A
positive level is stock on hand and a negative level is backorders (Axsäter, 2015, p. 40).

<WorkedExample id="ex-inventory-position">

A station with nothing on hand, 2 units backordered and 12 on order has an inventory level of $-2$ and
an inventory position of 10. A station with 5 on hand and 7 on order has a level of 5 and a position
of 12.

</WorkedExample>

<Check ref="maxima:inventory-position" />

<Lemma id="lem-level-after-lead-time" name="Level after a lead time">

Let $D(t, t']$ be the demand in the interval $(t, t']$. Then for every $t$,

$$
\mathit{IL}(t + L) = \mathit{IP}(t) - D(t, t + L] .
$$

</Lemma>

<Proof>

Every order outstanding at time $t$ was placed at or before $t$, so it has arrived by $t + L$. No order
placed after $t$ has arrived by $t + L$. The level at $t + L$ is therefore the level at $t$, plus all
the stock on order at $t$, less the demand in between. The first two terms make up $\mathit{IP}(t)$.

</Proof>

<Check ref="maxima:level-after-lead-time" />

The lemma is the reason for the definition. The position at time $t$ determines the level a lead time
later, up to the demand in between, and nothing the station does after $t$ can change that level.

### Base-stock policies

<Definition id="def-base-stock" name="Base-stock policy">

A **base-stock policy** with **base-stock level** $S$ orders, at each review, the quantity that raises
the inventory position to $S$.

</Definition>

<Proposition id="prop-cycle-level" name="Level during a cycle">

Under a base-stock policy with level $S$, let a review occur at time $t$. Then for $0 \le \tau < R$,

$$
\mathit{IL}(t + L + \tau) = S - D(t, t + L + \tau] .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

After the review $\mathit{IP}(t) = S$. No order is placed in $(t, t + \tau]$, so the position falls with
the demand and $\mathit{IP}(t + \tau) = S - D(t, t + \tau]$. Applying
<Ref to="lem-level-after-lead-time" /> at time $t + \tau$ gives
$\mathit{IL}(t + \tau + L) = S - D(t, t + \tau] - D(t + \tau, t + \tau + L]$.

</Proof>

The order placed at time $t$ arrives at $t + L$, and the next order arrives at $t + L + R$. Between
those arrivals the level is $S$ less the demand over an interval that grows from $L$ to $R + L$. The
order placed at $t$ must therefore cover the demand until $t + R + L$.

<Definition id="def-protection-interval" name="Protection interval">

The **protection interval** is $R + L$, the time from a review until the order placed at the next review
arrives.

</Definition>

<WorkedExample id="ex-protection-interval">

For the Shop the protection interval is $25/24$ days. The demand over the lead time is Poisson with mean
$\lambda L = 10$, and the demand over the protection interval is Poisson with mean
$\lambda (R + L) = 125/12 \approx 10.42$.

</WorkedExample>

  maxima:protection-interval

### The base-stock level

Let $N_u$ denote the demand over an interval of length $u$, which is Poisson with mean $\lambda u$, and
let $F_u$ be its distribution function. Define

$$
g_u(S) = h\,E\big[(S - N_u)^+\big] + p\,E\big[(N_u - S)^+\big] .
$$

<Theorem id="thm-base-stock" name="Base-stock level">

Under a base-stock policy with level $S$:

1. the expected rate of cost at a time $\tau$ after an order arrives, $0 \le \tau < R$, is
   $g_{L + \tau}(S)$;
2. for each $u$, the function $g_u$ is minimised by $S_u$, the smallest $S$ with
   $F_u(S) \ge p / (p + h)$;
3. the long-run average cost $\bar g(S) = \frac1R \int_0^R g_{L + \tau}(S)\,d\tau$ is minimised by the
   smallest $S$ with $\frac1R \int_0^R F_{L + \tau}(S)\,d\tau \ge p / (p + h)$, and this level $S^*$
   satisfies $S_L \le S^* \le S_{R + L}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By <Ref to="prop-cycle-level" /> the level at that time is $S - N$, where $N$ is the demand over an
interval of length $L + \tau$. The stock on hand is $(S - N)^+$ and the backorders are $(N - S)^+$,
which gives (1).

The function $g_u$ is the cost of <Ref to="lem-overage-underage" /> without its constant term, with
overage cost $h$ and underage cost $p$. As in the proof of <Ref to="thm-critical-ratio" />,

$$
g_u(S + 1) - g_u(S) = (h + p)\,F_u(S) - p ,
$$

which does not decrease with $S$ and is first non-negative at $S_u$. This gives (2).

Every cycle has the same distribution, so the long-run average cost is the mean of the cost rate over a
cycle, $\bar g(S)$. Averaging the last display over $\tau$ gives the condition in (3). More demand
arrives in a longer interval, so $F_u(S)$ decreases as $u$ grows, and the average of $F_{L + \tau}(S)$
lies between $F_{R + L}(S)$ and $F_L(S)$. Hence the average reaches $p/(p+h)$ no sooner than $F_L$ does
and no later than $F_{R + L}$ does.

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:cost-rate-definition
  maxima:cost-rate
" />

The ratio $p / (p + h)$ is the critical ratio of chapter 5 with $c_u = p$ and $c_o = h$. The optimal
level is a quantile of demand at that ratio, over an interval between the lead time and the protection
interval. When reviews are frequent, $R$ is small beside $L$ and the two bounds are close.

<Remark id="rem-base-stock-optimal">

Two related results are stated here without proof. With no fixed order cost, a base-stock policy is
optimal among all policies (Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 124–129). In a model that charges the holding and
backorder costs once in each review period, at its end, the optimal level is $S_{R + L}$ exactly (Snyder
and Shen, 2019, pp. 105–113; Axsäter, 2015, p. 96).

</Remark>

<WorkedExample id="ex-base-stock-shop">

For the Shop the critical ratio is $10/11 \approx 0.909$. Over the lead time, $F_L(13) = 0.8645$ and
$F_L(14) = 0.9165$, so $S_L = 14$. Over the protection interval, $F_{R+L}(14) = 0.8930$ and
$F_{R+L}(15) = 0.9352$, so $S_{R+L} = 15$. The average of $F_{L+\tau}(14)$ over a cycle is $0.9051$,
just below the ratio, so $S^* = 15$. The average costs are

| Level $S$ | 14 | 15 | 16 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\bar g(S)$, a day | 6.18 | 6.14 | 6.52 |

The level 15 exceeds the mean demand over the protection interval by 4.58 units. Chapter 7 calls that
excess the safety stock.

</WorkedExample>

<Check refs="
  maxima:base-stock-bounds
  maxima:average-cost
" />

<WorkedExample id="ex-on-hand" name="Ordering on the stock on hand">

Consider the rule that orders, at each review, the quantity that raises the stock on hand to $S$. Let the
demand be steady at rate $\lambda$, and let the stock first fall below $S$ just after a review. The next
review finds a shortfall of $\lambda R$ and orders it. Nothing arrives for a lead time, so the review
after that finds a shortfall of $2\lambda R$ and orders all of it again. The $k$-th review orders
$k \lambda R$, and the $m = L / R$ reviews of the first lead time order

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{m} k\,\lambda R = \frac{m + 1}{2}\,\lambda L
$$

in total, against a demand of $\lambda L$. For the Shop $m = 24$: the rule orders 125 units to replace 10.
The inventory position counts each unit on order once, and a base-stock policy orders exactly the
demand since the last review.

</WorkedExample>

## In the simulator

The `classic.reorder` template with the following parameters is the Shop of
<Ref to="asm-shop-lead-time" />. Its reviews are hourly by default. The run lasts 60 days and starts with
15 units on hand.

<Scenario template="classic.reorder" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.reorder {
  random = true,
  demand = 10,
  lead_time = days(1),
  order_cost = 0,
  initial = 15,
  duration = days(60),
}
```

`ops.order_up_to { level = S }` is the base-stock policy of <Ref to="def-base-stock" />. Every `ops`
target for a review is compared with the inventory position. The reference policy has $S^* = 15$:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.reorder
return ops.policy {
  review = { target = ops.order_up_to { level = 15000 } },
}
```

Compare it in **Batch**, over 200 replications, with a policy of level 16:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.reorder
return ops.policy {
  review = { target = ops.order_up_to { level = 16000 } },
}
```

<Predicted>

| Cost a day | Model $\bar g(S)$ | Simulator, 200 replications |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $S = 14$ | 6.18 | $6.16 \pm 0.11$ |
| $S = 15$ | 6.14 | $6.14 \pm 0.08$ |
| $S = 16$ | 6.52 | $6.52 \pm 0.06$ |

<Check refs="
  test:on the Shop with a 1-day lead time, over 200 replications of 60 days, ordering up to 14, 15 and 16 costs about 6.16, 6.14 and 6.52 a day, and the paired differences from 15 have less than half the standard deviation of independent replications
  test:the reorder template's base-stock reference for the Shop with a 1-day lead time orders up to 15 and expects 6.20 a day over 60 days
" />

</Predicted>

The run begins with the whole base-stock level on hand and nothing on order, so the stock on the first
day is above its long-run distribution. The template computes the expected cost of the 60-day run
exactly, start included, as 6.20 a day at $S = 15$. The comparison uses common random numbers
(<Ref to="def-crn" />), so both policies face the same demand. The paired differences have less than half
the standard deviation that independent replications would give. The interval for the difference
between levels 16 and 15, $0.39 \pm 0.04$, excludes zero.

The rule of <Ref to="ex-on-hand" /> needs plain Lua, since it ignores the inventory position:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.reorder
return {
  on_review = function(ctx)
    local short = 15000 - ctx.here.stock.Goods
    if short > 0 then
      ctx.order("Goods", short)
    end
  end,
}
```

Over 100 replications of 30 days it costs about 100 a day, more than fifteen times the cost of the
base-stock policy. The stock chart shows the stock rising far above 15 as the repeated orders arrive.

<Check ref="test:on the Shop with a 1-day lead time, ordering up to 15 from the stock on hand costs about 100 a day over 100 replications of 30 days, more than fifteen times the cost of ordering from the inventory position" />

## Case study: double dispatch

The `two-trains` starter has two trains on one line from a mine to a factory and a dome. Under
balancing, each train responds to the stock it finds at a station and takes no account of the cargo that
the other train is bringing. Both respond to the same shortage, and one station is oversupplied while the
other waits. This is the error of <Ref to="ex-on-hand" />: a decision based on the stock on hand, where
the inventory position would count the cargo on its way.

<Scenario starter="two-trains" />

Drain-and-fill with roles removes most of the unmet demand:

```lua runnable scenario=two-trains fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual {
    Mine = "supply",
    Factory = "demand",
    Dome = "demand",
  },
  target = { supply = ops.drain {}, demand = ops.fill {} },
}
```

[ops.lookahead](/docs/ops/lookahead) adds the inventory position to this policy. It reserves cargo for
the stations further along the line, and the other train counts the reserved cargo in each station's
position:

```lua runnable scenario=two-trains
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual {
    Mine = "supply",
    Factory = "demand",
    Dome = "demand",
  },
  target = { supply = ops.drain {}, demand = ops.fill {} },
  plan = ops.lookahead {},
}
```

Over 100 replications, roles alone leave less than a tenth of the baseline's unmet demand. With
lookahead added, the unmet demand is higher than with roles alone, and the share of distance travelled
empty is more than three times as high.

<Check ref="test:on two-trains over 100 replications, roles leave less than a tenth of the balancing baseline's unmet demand, and adding lookahead leaves more unmet demand than roles alone with over three times the empty running" />

The policy with lookahead counts the cargo on its way and omits the other half of this chapter. At the
mine a train loads what the stations ahead are short of at that moment. The dome is four hours away and
consumes 7 units in that time, and the factory, 100 minutes away, consumes almost 3. A load sized for the
present shortfall is a base-stock level with no allowance for the demand over the protection interval,
and it arrives too small.

<Check ref="maxima:two-trains-travel" />

## Where the simulator differs

- **Periodic review.** The simulator reviews on a schedule, one minute into each review period.
  Continuous review, which many texts assume, corresponds to a short review period.
- **Costs as they accrue.** The simulator charges the holding and backorder costs each minute on the
  stock on hand and the backorders, which is the cost rate of <Ref to="thm-base-stock" />. The
  template's reference level is $S_{R+L}$, the level for costs charged at the end of each period.
- **Lost sales.** The results of this chapter assume backorders. When unmet demand is lost and the lead
  time is positive, a base-stock policy is no longer optimal and the optimal policy has no simple form
  (Axsäter, 2015, pp. 97–99).

## Exercises

Each exercise uses the Shop's values in the table under
[The Shop with a lead time](#the-shop-with-a-lead-time), except where it states otherwise.

1. A station has nothing on hand, 4 units backordered and 12 on order. Find its inventory level and its
   inventory position, and the order that a base-stock policy with $S = 15$ places.

<Answer>

The level is $0 - 4 = -4$ and the position is $-4 + 12 = 8$. The policy orders $15 - 8 = 7$ units.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-position" />

</Answer>

2. Let the lead time be 2 days. Find $S_L$ and $S_{R+L}$, and decide which of them minimises the average
   cost $\bar g$.

<Answer>

The demand over the lead time has mean 20, and $F_L(25) = 0.8878 < 10/11 \le F_L(26) = 0.9221$, so
$S_L = 26$. Over the protection interval the mean is 20.42 and $S_{R+L} = 27$. The average of
$F_{L+\tau}(26)$ over a cycle is $0.9147 \ge 10/11$, so $S^* = 26$, with $\bar g(26) = 8.47$ against
$\bar g(27) = 8.53$. Here the lower bound of <Ref to="thm-base-stock" /> is attained.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-two-day-lead" />

</Answer>

3. Approximate the demand over the Shop's protection interval by a normal distribution with the same
   mean and variance. Use <Ref to="prop-normal-demand" /> to estimate the base-stock level and the cost
   of uncertainty, with $c_o = h$ and $c_u = p$.

<Answer>

The mean is $125/12 \approx 10.42$ and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{125/12} \approx 3.227$. With
$\Phi(z^*) = 10/11$, $z^* = 1.335$ and the level is $10.42 + 1.335 \times 3.227 = 14.73$, which rounds up
to the exact level of 15. The cost of uncertainty is $(h + p)\,\sigma\,\phi(z^*) = 11 \times 3.227 \times
0.1636 = 5.81$ a day, against the exact $g_{R+L}(15) = 6.16$.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-normal-approximation" />

</Answer>

4. Let the demand be 4 units a day, with reviews every 6 hours and a lead time of 1 day. Compute
   $S_{R+L}$. Then set the template's `demand = 4`, `review_period = hours(6)` and `duration = days(30)`,
   remove `initial`, and run the reference policy with that level over 100 replications.

<Answer>

The demand over the protection interval of $5/4$ days has mean 5, and
$F(7) = 0.8666 < 10/11 \le F(8) = 0.9319$, so $S_{R+L} = 8$. The template's exact expected cost for the
30-day run is 4.81 a day, and the mean cost over 100 replications agrees with it within its confidence
interval.

<Check refs="
  maxima:shorter-lead-time
  test:on classic.reorder with a 1-day lead time the reference orders up to 8 and expects 4.81 a day, and over 100 replications the simulated cost agrees within its interval
" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

On a rail line a train's visit is a review, and the time for cargo to reach a station is a lead time. A
train that considers only a station's stock, and omits the cargo that another train is bringing, makes
the error of ordering on the stock on hand. Stations that are far apart, or served by few trains, have
long protection intervals and need more stock to last between visits.

</GameNote>
