## What you will learn

- To write down the expected cost of a single order placed before random demand.
- To find the best order by comparing the cost of one unit too many with one unit too
  few, and to read that comparison as a probability, the critical ratio.
- To run the classic newsvendor scenario, and to see in a batch comparison why the cost
  curve is lopsided.

## The problem

Some decisions are made once, before the uncertainty they face is resolved. A bakery
bakes before the shop opens. A stall buys newspapers at dawn. A depot stocks spare parts
for a single season. What is left over at the end is worth little or nothing, and a
customer who finds the shelf empty buys elsewhere.

Order too much and you pay for stock that is wasted. Order too little and you forgo
sales you could have made. Neither mistake can be put right afterwards, so the question
is how to balance them before demand is known. Operations research calls this the
newsvendor problem, after the newspaper stall. It is the simplest setting in which
uncertainty changes the right decision, and the reasoning it teaches runs through the
rest of this part (Axsäter, 2015, pp. 95–97; Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 90–101).

## The model

Let one unit cost $c$ to buy, and let each unit of demand that finds no stock cost $p$,
the loss of an unmade sale. Demand $D$ is random with a known distribution. Order $Q$
units, and the period costs $cQ$ for the purchase plus $p$ for each unit of demand above
$Q$. The expected cost is

$$
C(Q) = cQ + p\,\mathbb{E}\left[(D - Q)^+\right],
$$

<Check ref="maxima:expected-cost" />

where $(x)^+$ means $\max(x, 0)$.

### One more unit

Instead of minimising $C$ directly, ask what one more unit changes. Its purchase adds
$c$ for certain. It saves $p$ only if demand turns out to be above $Q$, which happens
with probability $P(D > Q)$. For whole units, the change is

$$
C(Q + 1) - C(Q) = c - p\,P(D > Q).
$$

<Check ref="maxima:marginal-cost" />

As $Q$ grows, $P(D > Q)$ shrinks, so the change starts negative and turns positive.
The best order is the first $Q$ at which adding a unit stops paying: the smallest $Q$
with

$$
P(D \le Q) \ge \frac{p - c}{p}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:optimality-condition" />

### The critical ratio

The right-hand side is the critical ratio. It is often written with two named costs:
the underage cost $c_u$, what a unit too few gives up, here $p - c$; and the overage
cost $c_o$, what a unit too many wastes, here $c$. Then the ratio is

$$
\frac{c_u}{c_u + c_o} = \frac{p - c}{p}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:critical-ratio" />

The ratio says how much protection is worth buying. When a lost sale costs much more
than a wasted unit, the ratio is close to 1 and the order covers nearly every outcome.
When the two costs are close, the order sits nearer the middle of the distribution.

### A worked case

Take demand of 5, 10, 15, 20 or 25 units with weights 1, 2, 3, 2 and 1, a unit cost of
2 and a lost-demand cost of 5. The critical ratio is $3/5$. Demand is at most 10 with
probability $1/3$ and at most 15 with probability $2/3$, so 15 is the smallest order
that reaches the ratio.

<Check ref="maxima:best-order" />

The expected shortfall at 15 is $20/9$ units, and the expected cost is

$$
C(15) = 2 \times 15 + 5 \times \frac{20}{9} = \frac{370}{9} \approx 41.11.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:best-cost" />

The cost curve is not symmetric. Ordering 10 costs $430/9 \approx 47.78$, while ordering
20 costs $385/9 \approx 42.78$. Falling short is dearer than overshooting, because a lost
sale costs more than a wasted unit.

<Check ref="maxima:cost-either-side" />

## In the simulator

The `classic.newsvendor` template builds this problem as a scenario. One station stores
the product with unlimited room. Each day's demand is drawn from a discrete
distribution and spread over the day, and demand that finds no stock is lost. Stock
expires at each daily review, so nothing carries over. At the review the station orders
from an outside supplier with no lead time, and the order arrives before that day's
demand. The defaults are the worked case above, over 30 days.

<Scenario template="classic.newsvendor" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.newsvendor {}
```

The reference policy orders up to the critical-ratio quantity at every review. Since
the leftovers have just expired, that means ordering exactly 15 units:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.newsvendor
return ops.policy {
  review = { target = ops.order_up_to { level = 15000 } },
}
```

Amounts in policies are in thousandths of a unit, so 15 units is `15000`. A test runs
the template with this policy for 400 seeds, and checks that the analytic 41.11 lies
inside the 99% confidence interval of the simulated mean cost per day.

<Check ref="test:reaches the analytic expected cost at the critical-ratio quantity over 400 seeds" />

To see the lopsided curve, open **Batch**, tick **Compare**, and compare the reference
policy with copies ordering 10 and 20. The total cost metric rises on both sides of 15,
by more below it than above: over seeds 1 to 100 the three cost about 41, 43 and 47.5 a
day, each within a unit of the model's 41.11, 42.78 and 47.78.

<Check ref="test:on classic.newsvendor over seeds 1 to 100 ordering 15 costs about 41 a day, 20 about 43 and 10 about 47.5, each within a unit of the model" />

## Where the simulator differs

- **Timing.** Demand arrives through the day rather than all at once. Only the day's
  total affects the cost, so the expected cost is the same, but the station runs out
  part way through a busy day rather than at its end.
- **Whole cost units.** Costs are whole numbers per unit, so an example with prices in
  fractions needs scaling.
- **No salvage.** Unsold stock is worth nothing. It appears in the
  [expired stock](/docs/metrics#expired-stock) metric, not as a cost, because its cost
  was paid when it was bought.

## Exercises

1. With the worked case's demand and costs, how many units are left over on average
   when you order 15?

<Answer>

On average $20/9 \approx 2.22$ units, from $\mathbb{E}\left[(15 - D)^+\right]$: 10 left
with probability $1/9$ and 5 left with probability $2/9$.

<Check ref="maxima:expected-leftover" />

</Answer>

2. Suppose unsold units can be sold off for 1 each at the end of the day. How does the
   critical ratio change, and what is the best order for the worked case's demand?

<Answer>

A wasted unit now costs $c - s = 1$ instead of 2, while a lost sale still gives up
$p - c = 3$. The ratio becomes $3 / (3 + 1) = 3/4$. Demand is at most 15 with
probability $2/3$ and at most 20 with probability $8/9$, so the best order rises to 20.

<Check ref="maxima:salvage" />

</Answer>

3. Demand is instead roughly normal, with mean 100 and standard deviation 20, and the
   costs are as in the worked case. About how many units should be ordered?

<Answer>

The order is the $3/5$ quantile of demand, $100 + 20z$ with $z \approx 0.2533$, which is
about 105.07 units.

<Check ref="maxima:normal-demand" />

</Answer>

4. Raise the template's `lost_cost` to 10. What is the best order now, and what does it
   cost a day against ordering 15 or 25?

<Answer>

The critical ratio becomes $(10 - 2)/10 = 4/5$, and 20 is the smallest order with
$P(D \le Q) \ge 4/5$, since demand is at most 20 with probability $8/9$. Its expected cost
is $410/9 \approx 45.56$ a day; ordering 15 costs $470/9 \approx 52.22$, and ordering 25,
which covers every outcome, costs exactly 50. Over seeds 1 to 100 the simulator agrees:
about 45.8 for 20, and more for both 15 and 25.

<Check ref="maxima:lost-cost-ten" />

<Check ref="test:on classic.newsvendor with a lost-demand cost of 10, ordering 20 costs about 45.8 a day over seeds 1 to 100, less than ordering 15 or 25" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

A rail line has no stock that expires, but the newsvendor's trade-off appears whenever
a train leaves a station for a long round trip. Whatever the train leaves behind has to
last until it returns. Leaving too little starves the consumers there. Leaving too much
fills storage that the next delivery, or another station, could have used. Chapter 6
returns to this as the protection interval.

</GameNote>
