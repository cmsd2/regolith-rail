## What you will learn

- To formulate the expected cost of a single order placed before a random demand is known.
- To prove that the expected cost is convex, and that the optimal order is the quantile of demand at the
  critical ratio.
- To compute the optimal order and its cost for discrete and for continuous demand.
- To express the optimal cost under normal demand through the standard normal loss function, and to
  identify the part of it that uncertainty causes.

## The problem

Chapter 4 chose an order quantity for a demand that was known. This chapter keeps the choice of a
quantity and makes the demand random. It removes everything else. There is one period, and one order placed
before the demand is observed. Stock left at the end of the period has no value,
and demand that finds no stock is lost.

An order that is too large wastes the units left over. An order that is too small loses demand. The
problem of choosing the order is the newsvendor problem, named for a seller of newspapers who buys the
day's copies in the morning (Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 90–101; Axsäter, 2015, pp. 95–96). Its solution
is the simplest case in which uncertainty changes the optimal decision, and chapter 6 reduces a problem
with many periods to it.

## The model

### Expected cost

<Assumption id="asm-newsvendor" name="The newsvendor">

1. There is one period. The demand in the period is a random variable $D \ge 0$ with distribution
   function $F$.
2. The order $Q \ge 0$ is chosen before the demand is observed, and arrives before the period begins.
3. Each unit ordered costs $c$.
4. Each unit of demand that is not met costs $p$, where $p > c > 0$. Stock left at the end of the period
   has no value.

</Assumption>

<Definition id="def-newsvendor-cost" name="Expected cost">

With $(y)^+ = \max(y, 0)$, the **expected cost** of the order $Q$ is

$$
C(Q) = cQ + p\,E\big[(D - Q)^+\big] .
$$

</Definition>

The term $E[(D - Q)^+]$ is the expected shortfall. The cost can be divided differently, into a part that
no order can avoid and the costs of the two kinds of error.

<Lemma id="lem-overage-underage" name="Overage and underage">

Let $c_o = c$ and $c_u = p - c$. Then

$$
C(Q) = c\,E[D] + c_o\,E\big[(Q - D)^+\big] + c_u\,E\big[(D - Q)^+\big] .
$$

</Lemma>

<Proof>

By the identity $(y)^+ - (-y)^+ = y$ used for <Ref to="lem-conservation" />, with $y = Q - D$,
$Q = D + (Q - D)^+ - (D - Q)^+$. Multiplying by $c$ and taking expectations expresses $cQ$ in terms of
the two expected errors, and adding $p\,E[(D - Q)^+]$ gives the result.

</Proof>

The **overage cost** $c_o$ is the cost of a unit ordered and not demanded. The **underage cost** $c_u$
is the cost of a unit demanded and not ordered: the penalty $p$, less the purchase cost $c$ that was not
paid (Axsäter, 2015, p. 95).

<Proposition id="prop-newsvendor-convex" name="Convexity">

The expected cost $C$ is a convex function of $Q$.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

For each fixed value $d$ of the demand, the function $Q \mapsto (d - Q)^+$ is convex. An expectation of
convex functions is convex, and so is the sum of a convex function and the linear function $cQ$.

</Proof>

### The critical ratio

<Theorem id="thm-critical-ratio" name="Critical ratio, discrete demand">

Let $D$ take values in the non-negative integers, and let $Q$ be an integer. Then

$$
C(Q + 1) - C(Q) = c - p\,P(D > Q),
$$

and the smallest $Q$ with

$$
F(Q) \ \ge\ \frac{p - c}{p} = \frac{c_u}{c_u + c_o}
$$

minimises $C$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

One more unit costs $c$. It reduces the shortfall by one unit when $D > Q$ and has no effect otherwise,
since $(D - Q - 1)^+ - (D - Q)^+$ equals $-1$ when $D \ge Q + 1$ and 0 when $D \le Q$. Taking
expectations gives the first equation.

The probability $P(D > Q)$ does not increase with $Q$, so the difference $C(Q + 1) - C(Q)$ does not
decrease. Hence $C$ falls while the difference is negative and does not fall after, and the first $Q$ at
which $c - p\,P(D > Q) \ge 0$ is a minimiser. That condition is $1 - F(Q) \le c / p$.

</Proof>

The number $(p - c)/p$ is the **critical ratio**. The optimal order is the quantile of the demand at the
critical ratio: the smallest quantity that covers the demand with at least that probability. When the
penalty $p$ is large relative to the unit cost, the ratio is near 1 and the order covers almost every
demand. When $p$ is near $c$, the ratio is near 0 and the order is small.

<WorkedExample id="ex-stand" name="A newspaper stand">

A stand's daily demand is 5, 10, 15, 20 or 25 units, with probabilities $\tfrac19, \tfrac29, \tfrac39,
\tfrac29, \tfrac19$. A unit costs $c = 2$ and a unit of lost demand costs $p = 5$, so the critical ratio
is $3/5$. Since $F(10) = 1/3$ and $F(15) = 2/3$, the optimal order is $Q^* = 15$. The expected shortfall
at 15 is $\tfrac29 \times 5 + \tfrac19 \times 10 = 20/9$ units, and

$$
C(15) = 2 \times 15 + 5 \times \frac{20}{9} = \frac{370}{9} \approx 41.11 .
$$

The cost is not symmetric about the optimum: $C(10) = 430/9 \approx 47.78$ and
$C(20) = 385/9 \approx 42.78$. An order 5 units too small costs 6.67 more, and an order 5 units too
large costs 1.67 more. The demand is symmetric about 15, so the asymmetry comes from the costs:
$c_u = 3$ exceeds $c_o = 2$.

</WorkedExample>

<Theorem id="thm-critical-ratio-continuous" name="Critical ratio, continuous demand">

Let $F$ be continuous. Then $C$ is differentiable with

$$
C'(Q) = c - p\,\big(1 - F(Q)\big),
$$

and $Q^*$ minimises $C$ if and only if $F(Q^*) = (p - c)/p$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The expectation of a non-negative random variable is the integral of its tail probabilities, so

$$
E\big[(D - Q)^+\big] = \int_Q^\infty \big(1 - F(t)\big)\,dt ,
$$

whose derivative with respect to $Q$ is $-(1 - F(Q))$. This gives $C'$. By
<Ref to="prop-newsvendor-convex" /> $C$ is convex, so its minimisers are the points where $C'$ is zero.

</Proof>

<WorkedExample id="ex-uniform-demand">

Let $D$ be uniform on $[0, 30]$, with $c = 2$ and $p = 5$. Then $F(Q) = Q / 30$, and $F(Q^*) = 3/5$ gives
$Q^* = 18$. The expected shortfall is $\int_Q^{30} (1 - t/30)\,dt = (30 - Q)^2 / 60$, which is 2.4 at
$Q^*$, and $C(18) = 36 + 5 \times 2.4 = 48$.

</WorkedExample>

### Normal demand

Under normal demand the optimal order and its cost have closed forms. Let $Z$ be a standard normal random
variable with density $\phi$ and distribution function $\Phi$.

<Definition id="def-loss-function" name="Standard normal loss function">

The **standard normal loss function** is $G(z) = E\big[(Z - z)^+\big]$, the expected amount by which $Z$
exceeds $z$.

</Definition>

<Lemma id="lem-loss-function">

$$
G(z) = \phi(z) - z\,\big(1 - \Phi(z)\big), \qquad G'(z) = \Phi(z) - 1 .
$$

</Lemma>

<Proof>

By definition $G(z) = \int_z^\infty t\,\phi(t)\,dt - z \int_z^\infty \phi(t)\,dt$. The density satisfies
$\phi'(t) = -t\,\phi(t)$, so the first integral is $\phi(z)$. The second is $1 - \Phi(z)$. Differentiating
gives $G'(z) = -z\,\phi(z) - (1 - \Phi(z)) + z\,\phi(z) = \Phi(z) - 1$.

</Proof>

<Proposition id="prop-normal-demand" name="Normal demand">

Let $D$ be normal with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and let $z^*$ satisfy
$\Phi(z^*) = (p - c)/p$. Then the optimal order and its expected cost are

$$
Q^* = \mu + z^* \sigma, \qquad C(Q^*) = c\,\mu + p\,\sigma\,\phi(z^*) .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

Write $D = \mu + \sigma Z$ and $Q = \mu + z\sigma$. Then $(D - Q)^+ = \sigma\,(Z - z)^+$, so the expected
shortfall is $\sigma\,G(z)$ and $C(Q) = c\,(\mu + z\sigma) + p\,\sigma\,G(z)$. By
<Ref to="thm-critical-ratio-continuous" /> the optimal $z$ satisfies $\Phi(z^*) = (p - c)/p$, that is,
$1 - \Phi(z^*) = c / p$. Substituting <Ref to="lem-loss-function" />,

$$
C(Q^*) = c\,\mu + c\,z^* \sigma + p\,\sigma\,\phi(z^*) - p\,\sigma\,z^* \cdot \frac{c}{p}
= c\,\mu + p\,\sigma\,\phi(z^*) .
$$

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:loss-function
  maxima:normal-shortfall
  maxima:normal-optimum
" />

The cost has two parts. The part $c\,\mu$ is the cost of buying the mean demand, which would be the whole
cost if the demand were known. The part $p\,\sigma\,\phi(z^*)$ is the cost of uncertainty. It is
proportional to the standard deviation of the demand and does not depend on its mean (Snyder and Shen,
2019, pp. 97–99). A normal variable can be negative, so the model suits a demand whose mean is several
standard deviations above zero.

<WorkedExample id="ex-normal-demand">

Let $\mu = 100$ and $\sigma = 20$, with $c = 2$ and $p = 5$. The critical ratio is $0.6$, so
$z^* = 0.2533$ and $Q^* = 105.07$. With $\phi(z^*) = 0.3863$ the cost of uncertainty is
$5 \times 20 \times 0.3863 = 38.63$, and $C(Q^*) = 238.63$. Ordering the mean, $z = 0$, costs
$200 + 5 \times 20 \times G(0) = 239.89$, which is 1.26 more.

With $p = 10$ the critical ratio is $0.8$, so $z^* = 0.8416$ and $Q^* = 116.83$. The cost of uncertainty
is $10 \times 20 \times 0.2800 = 55.99$ at $Q^*$ and $79.79$ at the mean, a difference of 23.80. The
value of ordering the right quantile grows as the critical ratio moves away from $1/2$.

</WorkedExample>

<Check ref="maxima:normal-example" />

<Remark id="rem-salvage" name="Salvage value">

If each unit left over can be sold for $v < c$, the overage cost becomes $c_o = c - v$ and the underage
cost is unchanged, so the critical ratio is $(p - c)/(p - v)$. For the stand of <Ref to="ex-stand" />
with $v = 1$ the ratio is $3/4$. Since $F(15) = 2/3$ and $F(20) = 8/9$, the optimal order rises to 20.

</Remark>

## In the simulator

The `classic.newsvendor` template is the stand of <Ref to="ex-stand" />, repeated for 30 days. One
station stores the product. Each day's demand is drawn from the distribution of the example, demand that
finds no stock is lost, and stock expires at the daily review. At the review the station orders from a
supplier with no lead time, and the order arrives before the day's demand.

<Scenario template="classic.newsvendor" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.newsvendor {}
```

`ops.order_up_to { level = S }` sets a target of $S$ at every review, in thousandths of a unit. The stock
has just expired, so the policy orders exactly $S$. The reference policy orders $Q^* = 15$:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.newsvendor
return ops.policy {
  review = { target = ops.order_up_to { level = 15000 } },
}
```

Each day is one period of the model, so the mean of **Total cost** per day over a batch estimates $C(Q)$.
In **Batch**, run 100 replications of the reference policy and of copies with `level = 10000` and
`level = 20000`.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Demand through the day.** The simulator spreads each day's demand over the day. Only the day's total
  affects the cost, so the expected cost is that of the model, although the station can run out before
  the day ends.
- **Whole cost units.** The simulator's unit costs are whole numbers, so an example with fractional costs
  must be rescaled.
- **Expired stock.** Stock left at the review appears under the metric
  [Expired stock](/docs/metrics#expired-stock) and carries no cost of its own, since its purchase cost was
  charged when it was ordered.

## Exercises

1. For the stand of <Ref to="ex-stand" />, compute the expected number of units left over when the order
   is 15.

<Answer>

$E[(15 - D)^+] = \tfrac19 \times 10 + \tfrac29 \times 5 = 20/9 \approx 2.22$ units.

<Check ref="maxima:expected-leftover" />

</Answer>

2. Let the demand be exponential with mean 15, so that $F(Q) = 1 - e^{-Q/15}$, with $c = 2$ and $p = 5$.
   Find the optimal order.

<Answer>

By <Ref to="thm-critical-ratio-continuous" />, $1 - e^{-Q^*/15} = 3/5$, so
$Q^* = 15 \ln(5/2) \approx 13.74$. The optimal order is below the mean demand, because the median of an
exponential distribution is below its mean and the critical ratio is close to $1/2$.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-exponential" />

</Answer>

3. Let the demand be normal with $\mu = 100$ and $\sigma = 20$, and let $c = 2$ and $p = 4$. Find the
   optimal order and the cost of uncertainty.

<Answer>

The critical ratio is $(4 - 2)/4 = 1/2$, so $z^* = 0$ and $Q^* = \mu = 100$. By
<Ref to="prop-normal-demand" /> the cost of uncertainty is
$p\,\sigma\,\phi(0) = 4 \times 20 \times 0.3989 = 31.92$.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-half-ratio" />

</Answer>

4. In the template, set `lost_cost = 10`. Compute the critical ratio and the optimal order, and the
   expected costs of orders of 15, 20 and 25. Then compare the three orders in a batch of 100
   replications.

<Answer>

The critical ratio is $(10 - 2)/10 = 4/5$. Since $F(15) = 2/3$ and $F(20) = 8/9$, the optimal order is
20, with $C(20) = 410/9 \approx 45.56$. The other orders cost $C(15) = 470/9 \approx 52.22$ and
$C(25) = 50$. The batch gives a mean cost of about 45.6 a day for an order of 20, and more for 15 and
for 25.

<Check refs="

" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

Stock on a rail line does not expire, but the newsvendor's trade-off arises whenever a train leaves a
station for a long round trip. What the train leaves must last until it returns. Too little leaves the
consumers short. Too much occupies storage and cargo that another station could have used. Chapter 6
treats the time until the next delivery as the period of this chapter.

</GameNote>
