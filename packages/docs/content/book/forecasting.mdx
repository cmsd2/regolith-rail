## What you will learn

- To forecast demand with a moving average and with exponential smoothing, and to see
  both as weighted averages of the past.
- To work out how far each forecast lags behind demand that is growing, and how Holt's
  method removes the lag.
- To measure forecast error, and to size the safety stock that a lagging forecast needs.

## The problem

Every policy in Part II so far was told the demand distribution. In practice nobody tells
you. A store sees what customers took yesterday and last week, and has to judge what
they will take tomorrow. That judgement is a forecast, and every order-up-to level is
only as good as the forecast behind it.

The simplest forecasts average the recent past. They smooth away noise, but they also
look backwards: when demand is growing, an average of old demand is always too low, and a
store that orders from it runs short again and again (Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 6–17;
Axsäter, 2015, pp. 28–29).

## The model

Write demand on day $k$ as $D_k$, and the forecast for day $k$, made the day before, as
$F_k$.

### Moving averages

A moving average forecasts the next day as the average of the last $N$:
$F_k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} D_{k-j}$. If demand grows by $b$ a day,
$D_k = a + bk$, the forecast trails the demand it forecasts by

$$
D_k - F_k = \frac{b\,(N + 1)}{2}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:moving-average-lag" />

A 7-day average of demand growing by 1 a day is always 4 units short. Averaging over a
whole season removes the season, because the high and low days balance out.

<Check ref="maxima:moving-average-lag" />

<Check ref="maxima:season-average" />

### Exponential smoothing

Exponential smoothing keeps one number and updates it each day, moving it part of the way
towards what was just seen:

$$
F_{k+1} = \alpha D_k + (1 - \alpha) F_k.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:smoothing-weights" />

Unrolled, the forecast is a weighted average of all past demand, with weight
$\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$ on the day $j$ days before the latest. The weights sum to 1, and the
average age of the data is $(1 - \alpha)/\alpha$ days, 4 days for $\alpha = 0.2$. A moving
average of $N = 2/\alpha - 1$ days, 9 days here, has the same average age.

<Check ref="maxima:smoothing-weights" />

<Check ref="maxima:equivalent-average" />

Under a steady trend, the error $D_{k+1} - F_{k+1}$ settles where it stops changing,
which gives

$$
D_{k+1} - F_{k+1} = \frac{b}{\alpha}, \qquad D_k - F_{k+1} = \frac{b\,(1 - \alpha)}{\alpha}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:smoothing-lag" />

With $b = 1$ and $\alpha = 0.2$, the forecast is 5 units below the day it forecasts, and
4 below the day just seen. A larger $\alpha$ lags less but passes more noise into the
forecast.

<Check ref="maxima:smoothing-lag" />

### Holt's method

Holt's method smooths a trend as well as a level, and forecasts the level plus the trend.
Started with no idea of the trend, its error dies away, while simple smoothing's settles
at $b/\alpha$.

<Check ref="maxima:holt" />

### Measuring error

Given errors $e_k = D_k - F_k$, the bias is their average, the mean absolute error the
average of their sizes, and the root mean squared error the square root of the average
square. For errors of 2, −1, 3, 0 and −2, the bias is 0.4, the mean absolute error 1.6 and
the root mean squared error about 1.90. A forecast with a steady positive bias, like
smoothing under growth, needs correcting, not just more safety stock around it.

<Check ref="maxima:error-measures" />

### What the lag costs

An order placed on day $k$ that must last three days, the lead time of 2 and one review,
faces demand $3D_k + 3b$. Ordering up to three days of the smoothed forecast plans for
$3(D_k - b/\alpha)$. The difference is

$$
\frac{3b}{\alpha} + 3b = 18 \text{ units}
$$

<Check ref="maxima:cover-shortfall" />

for $b = 1$ and $\alpha = 0.2$: without at least 18 units of safety stock, customers wait
every day.

## In the simulator

The `classic.forecasting` template has one store reviewed daily, a lead time of 2 days,
and demand that starts at 20 a day and grows by 1 each day, optionally with a season and
Poisson noise. Demand that finds no stock is backordered.

<Scenario template="classic.forecasting" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.forecasting { level = 20, trend = 1, noise = false }
```

<Check ref="test:starts demand at the level and raises it by the trend each day" />

The reference policy measures what the last day used from the fall in its inventory
position, smooths it, and orders up to three days of forecast plus a safety allowance. It
records its demand and forecast each day, so you can chart them together.

```lua runnable scenario=classic.forecasting
local ALPHA = 0.2
local COVER = 3
local SAFETY = 0

return {
  on_review = function(ctx)
    local here, memory = ctx.here, ctx.memory
    local position = (here.stock.Goods or 0) - (here.backorders and here.backorders.Goods or 0)
    for _, order in ipairs(here.on_order or {}) do
      if order.resource == "Goods" then
        position = position + order.amount
      end
    end
    if memory.after ~= nil then
      local used = memory.after - position
      if memory.forecast == nil then
        memory.forecast = used
      else
        memory.forecast = ALPHA * used + (1 - ALPHA) * memory.forecast
      end
      ctx.record("demand", used / 1000)
      ctx.record("forecast", memory.forecast / 1000)
    end
    local target = math.floor((memory.forecast or 0) * COVER + SAFETY + 0.5)
    local amount = 0
    if target > position then
      amount = target - position
      ctx.order("Goods", amount)
    end
    memory.after = position + amount
  end,
}
```

From day 40 on, each recorded forecast is within a unit of 5 below the day it forecasts
and 4 below the day just seen, as the model says.

<Check ref="test:smooths so that after warm-up each forecast trails its day's demand by trend over alpha, and the latest day by trend times one minus alpha over alpha" />

Set the template's `safety` parameter to see the lag's cost. With 17 units, customers
wait on every day from 40 to 59. With 18, they never do.

<Check ref="test:on classic.forecasting the smoothing policy leaves customers waiting on every day from 40 to 59 with 17 units of safety stock, and on none of them with 18" />

## Where the simulator differs

- **Demand is observed, not told.** The policy works out demand from its inventory
  position, which counts backorders, so demand that finds no stock is still seen. With
  lost sales it would not be: a store that runs dry records what was taken, not what was
  wanted, and its forecasts drift low.
- **Stepped, not smooth, growth.** Demand changes once a day, by the trend, rather than
  continuously.
- **Seasons are two flat halves.** The template's season raises demand for the first half
  and lowers it for the second, a simpler shape than most real seasons.

## Exercises

   how much safety stock does the three-day cover need?

<Answer>

The lag falls to $b/\alpha = 2$ units, and the shortfall to $3 \times 2 + 3 = 9$ units.

2. Demand grows by 2 a day and a store forecasts with a 9-day moving average. How far
   behind is the forecast?

<Answer>

$b(N + 1)/2 = 2 \times 10 / 2 = 10$ units.

<GameNote>

A colony's demand grows as it grows: more domes, more consumers. A dispatch rule that
aims for last week's usage keeps falling behind a growing colony, in the same way as
smoothing falls behind a trend. Rules that watch how fast a station's stock is falling,
not just how full it is, can see the growth coming.

</GameNote>
