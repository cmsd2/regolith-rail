## What you will learn

- To define the moving average and exponential smoothing, and to express smoothing as a weighted average
  of past demand.
- To prove the variance of each forecast under a constant mean demand, and its lag under a linear trend.
- To state Holt's method and prove that it follows a linear trend without lag.
- To compute the stock that an order-up-to level based on a forecast needs.

## The problem

Chapters 4 to 7 took the demand rate, or the distribution of the demand, as known. A station seldom
knows either. It observes the demand of each past period and must estimate the demand of the next
periods from those observations. The estimate is a forecast, and the order-up-to level of chapter 6
depends on it.

The simplest forecasts are averages of recent demand. An average over many periods varies little and
responds slowly when the mean demand changes. An average over few periods responds quickly and varies
more. This chapter measures both effects for the moving average and for exponential smoothing, and
treats a method that follows a trend (Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 6–17; Axsäter, 2015, pp. 9–16).

## The model

### Demand models and forecasts

<Definition id="def-demand-models" name="Demand models">

Let $d_t$ be the demand in period $t$. Under the **level model**, $d_t = a + \varepsilon_t$. Under the
**trend model**, $d_t = a + bt + \varepsilon_t$. In both, the $\varepsilon_t$ are independent random
variables with mean zero and variance $\sigma^2$, and $a$ and $b$ are constants that the forecaster does
not know.

</Definition>

<Definition id="def-forecast" name="Forecast and forecast error">

A **forecast** $f_{t+1}$ is an estimate of $d_{t+1}$ computed from $d_1, \dots, d_t$. Its **forecast
error** is $e_{t+1} = d_{t+1} - f_{t+1}$.

</Definition>

<Definition id="def-moving-average" name="Moving average">

The **moving average** over $M$ periods is

$$
\hat a_t = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} d_{t-j}, \qquad f_{t+1} = \hat a_t .
$$

</Definition>

<Definition id="def-exponential-smoothing" name="Exponential smoothing">

**Exponential smoothing** with **smoothing constant** $\alpha$, where $0 < \alpha < 1$, updates one number
in each period:

$$
\hat a_t = \alpha\,d_t + (1 - \alpha)\,\hat a_{t-1}, \qquad f_{t+1} = \hat a_t .
$$

</Definition>

Both definitions follow Axsäter (2015, pp. 11–12). The moving average stores $M$ demands. Exponential
smoothing stores only $\hat a_{t-1}$.

<Proposition id="prop-smoothing-weights" name="Smoothing as a weighted average">

For every $n \ge 0$,

$$
\hat a_t = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \alpha (1 - \alpha)^j\, d_{t-j} + (1 - \alpha)^{n+1}\, \hat a_{t-n-1} .
$$

The weights $\alpha (1 - \alpha)^j$, $j = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, sum to 1, and the mean age of the data under
these weights is $(1 - \alpha)/\alpha$ periods. A moving average over $M$ periods has a mean age of
$(M - 1)/2$ periods.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

Substituting the definition of $\hat a_{t-1}$ into that of $\hat a_t$, and repeating $n$ times, gives
the display. The weights form a geometric series with sum $\alpha / (1 - (1 - \alpha)) = 1$. The mean
age is $\sum_j j\,\alpha (1 - \alpha)^j$, and $\sum_j j x^j = x / (1 - x)^2$ with $x = 1 - \alpha$ gives
$(1 - \alpha)/\alpha$. The moving average gives weight $1/M$ to each of the ages $0, 1, \dots, M - 1$,
whose mean is $(M - 1)/2$.

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:moving-average
  maxima:exponential-smoothing
  maxima:smoothing-weights
  maxima:mean-age
" />

The two forecasts use data of the same mean age when $(M - 1)/2 = (1 - \alpha)/\alpha$, that is, when
$M = 2/\alpha - 1$. The next two results show that with this correspondence they also have the same
variance and the same lag.

### Variance under the level model

<Proposition id="prop-forecast-variance" name="Variance of a forecast">

Suppose the demand follows the level model, and suppose the smoothing has run long enough for the weight
on its initial value to be negligible. Then both forecasts have mean $a$, and

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat a_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{M} \ \ \text{for the moving average}, \qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\hat a_t) = \frac{\alpha}{2 - \alpha}\,\sigma^2 \ \ \text{for exponential smoothing}.
$$

The forecast error has mean zero and variance $\sigma^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat a_t)$.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

Each forecast is a weighted sum of independent demands with mean $a$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and the
weights sum to 1. Its mean is therefore $a$, and its variance is $\sigma^2$ multiplied by the sum of the
squared weights. For the moving average that sum is $M \times (1/M)^2 = 1/M$. For exponential smoothing
it is

$$
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \alpha^2 (1 - \alpha)^{2j} = \frac{\alpha^2}{1 - (1 - \alpha)^2} = \frac{\alpha}{2 - \alpha} .
$$

The demand $d_{t+1}$ is independent of $\hat a_t$, so the variance of their difference is the sum of
their variances.

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:forecast-variance
  maxima:squared-weights
" />

With $M = 2/\alpha - 1$ the two variances are equal, since $\alpha / (2 - \alpha) = 1/M$. A forecast with
a mean of $a$ is **unbiased**. Its variance falls as $M$ grows or as $\alpha$ falls.

### Lag under the trend model

<Proposition id="prop-forecast-lag" name="Lag behind a trend">

Suppose the demand follows the trend model with $\sigma = 0$, so that $d_t = a + bt$. Then the forecast
error of the moving average is

$$
e_{t+1} = \frac{b\,(M + 1)}{2},
$$

and the forecast error of exponential smoothing satisfies $e_{t+1} = (1 - \alpha)\,e_t + b$ and converges
to $b / \alpha$.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

The moving average is the mean of $d_t, d_{t-1}, \dots, d_{t-M+1}$, which is $d_t - b\,(M - 1)/2$. Since
$d_{t+1} = d_t + b$, the error is $b + b\,(M - 1)/2$.

For exponential smoothing, $e_{t+1} = d_{t+1} - \hat a_t = d_t + b - \hat a_{t-1} - \alpha\,(d_t -
\hat a_{t-1})$, and $d_t - \hat a_{t-1} = e_t$. Hence $e_{t+1} = (1 - \alpha)\,e_t + b$. The fixed point
of this recursion is $b / \alpha$, and

$$
e_{t+1} - \frac{b}{\alpha} = (1 - \alpha) \left(e_t - \frac{b}{\alpha}\right),
$$

so the distance from the fixed point shrinks by the factor $1 - \alpha$ in each period.

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:moving-average-lag
  maxima:smoothing-lag
" />

Under a rising trend both forecasts are too low by a constant amount, their **lag**. With
$M = 2/\alpha - 1$ the two lags are equal. The lag is a bias: the forecast error no longer has mean zero.

<WorkedExample id="ex-forecast-trade-off">

The variance and the lag move in opposite directions as the smoothing constant changes. For a trend of
$b = 1$ unit per period:

| Smoothing constant $\alpha$ | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Matching moving average $M$ | 19 | 9 | 4 |
| Variance of the forecast, as a multiple of $\sigma^2$ | 0.053 | 0.111 | 0.250 |
| Lag $b / \alpha$, units | 10 | 5 | 2.5 |

</WorkedExample>

<Check ref="maxima:trade-off" />

### Holt's method

<Definition id="def-holt" name="Holt's method">

**Holt's method** keeps an estimate $\hat a_t$ of the level in period $t$ and an estimate $\hat b_t$ of
the trend, with smoothing constants $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ between 0 and 1:

$$
\hat a_t = \alpha\,d_t + (1 - \alpha)\big(\hat a_{t-1} + \hat b_{t-1}\big), \qquad
\hat b_t = \gamma\,\big(\hat a_t - \hat a_{t-1}\big) + (1 - \gamma)\,\hat b_{t-1} .
$$

The forecast of the demand $k$ periods ahead is $f_{t+k} = \hat a_t + k\,\hat b_t$.

</Definition>

<Proposition id="prop-holt" name="Holt's method follows a trend">

Suppose $d_t = a + bt$. If $\hat a_{t-1} = a + b\,(t - 1)$ and $\hat b_{t-1} = b$, then
$\hat a_t = a + bt$ and $\hat b_t = b$, and every forecast $f_{t+k}$ equals $d_{t+k}$.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

The quantity $\hat a_{t-1} + \hat b_{t-1}$ equals $a + bt = d_t$, so $\hat a_t$ is a weighted average of
two copies of $d_t$. Then $\hat a_t - \hat a_{t-1} = b$, so $\hat b_t$ is a weighted average of two
copies of $b$.

</Proof>

Exact estimates therefore remain exact, where exponential smoothing keeps a lag of $b / \alpha$. From
inexact initial estimates the errors of Holt's method converge to zero (Axsäter, 2015, p. 15).

<WorkedExample id="ex-holt">

Let $d_t = 20 + t$, and start both methods from a level of 20, with Holt's trend estimate at zero. With
$\alpha = 0.2$ and $\gamma = 0.1$, the forecast error of Holt's method after 200 periods is below 0.01,
and the forecast error of exponential smoothing is 5.

</WorkedExample>

### Measuring forecast errors

<Definition id="def-error-measures" name="Measures of forecast error">

For forecast errors $e_1, \dots, e_n$, the **bias** is their mean, the **mean absolute deviation** is the
mean of $|e_t|$, and the **root mean squared error** is the square root of the mean of $e_t^2$.

</Definition>

<WorkedExample id="ex-error-measures">

For the errors $2, -1, 3, 0, -2$ the bias is $0.4$, the mean absolute deviation is $1.6$ and the root
mean squared error is $\sqrt{3.6} \approx 1.90$.

</WorkedExample>

<Check ref="maxima:error-measures" />

These are the usual measures (Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 15–16; Axsäter, 2015, pp. 25–26). A bias near
zero with a large root mean squared error indicates a forecast that is right on average and variable. A
bias that persists indicates a lag, and by <Ref to="prop-forecast-lag" /> a longer average increases it.

### Stock from a forecast

A base-stock policy needs the demand over the protection interval. Let the interval contain $n$ periods.
Exponential smoothing forecasts each of them as $\hat a_t$, so the order-up-to level after period $t$ is
$n\,\hat a_t$ plus a safety stock.

<Proposition id="prop-forecast-shortfall" name="Shortfall of a lagging forecast">

Let $d_t = a + bt$, and let $\hat a_t$ be the exponential smoothing estimate after its error has
converged. Then the demand in periods $t + 1, \dots, t + n$ exceeds $n\,\hat a_t$ by

$$
\frac{n\,b\,(1 - \alpha)}{\alpha} + \frac{b\,n\,(n + 1)}{2} .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

By <Ref to="prop-forecast-lag" />, $d_{t+1} - \hat a_t = b / \alpha$, so
$d_t - \hat a_t = b\,(1 - \alpha)/\alpha$. The demand in the $n$ periods is
$\sum_{k=1}^{n} (d_t + kb) = n\,d_t + b\,n\,(n + 1)/2$. Subtracting $n\,\hat a_t$ gives the result.

</Proof>

The first term is the lag of the estimate behind the latest demand, counted once for each period. The
second is the growth of the demand during the interval, which a forecast without a trend omits.

<WorkedExample id="ex-forecast-shortfall" name="The Shop with growing demand">

Let the Shop's demand begin at 10 units a day and grow by $b = 1$ a day. The Shop reviews daily, its lead
time is 2 days, and it forecasts by exponential smoothing with $\alpha = 0.2$. The protection interval is
$n = 3$ days, and the shortfall is

$$
\frac{3 \times 1 \times 0.8}{0.2} + \frac{1 \times 3 \times 4}{2} = 12 + 6 = 18\ \text{units}.
$$

An order-up-to level of $3\,\hat a_t$ plus a safety stock below 18 units leaves demand backordered in
every cycle. A safety stock of 18 units covers the shortfall exactly.

</WorkedExample>

<Check refs="

  maxima:cover-shortfall-shop
" />

<Remark id="rem-forecast-safety-stock">

When the forecast is unbiased and its errors in successive periods are independent with standard
deviation $\sigma_e$, the error over $n$ periods has standard deviation $\sigma_e \sqrt n$, and the safety
stock of <Ref to="prop-safety-stock-normal" /> is $z_\alpha\,\sigma_e \sqrt n$ (Axsäter, 2015, p. 28). The
safety stock then protects against the error of the forecast, which includes the variance of
<Ref to="prop-forecast-variance" />. A bias is better removed, by a method such as Holt's, than covered
by stock.

</Remark>

<Remark id="rem-seasonality">

A moving average over a whole number of seasons is unaffected by a seasonal pattern, since each part of
the season enters it with the same weight. Methods that estimate the pattern, such as Winters' method,
extend Holt's method with a third smoothed quantity (Axsäter, 2015, pp. 17–18).

</Remark>

## In the simulator

The `classic.forecasting` template with `level = 10` is the Shop of <Ref to="ex-forecast-shortfall" />:
daily reviews, a lead time of 2 days, backorders, and demand that begins at 10 units a day and grows by
1 a day. The template can add Poisson noise and a season.

<Scenario template="classic.forecasting" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.forecasting { level = 10, trend = 1, noise = false }
```

<Check ref="test:starts demand at the level and raises it by the trend each day" />

The reference policy is plain Lua. It computes the inventory position, takes the fall in the position
since the last review as the demand of the last day, and smooths that demand into `memory.forecast`. It
then orders up to `COVER` days of the forecast plus `SAFETY`, and records the demand and the forecast so
that the run view can chart them together.

```lua runnable scenario=classic.forecasting
local ALPHA = 0.2
local COVER = 3
local SAFETY = 0

return {
  on_review = function(ctx)
    local here, memory = ctx.here, ctx.memory
    local stock = here.stock.Goods or 0
    local backorders = here.backorders and here.backorders.Goods or 0
    local position = stock - backorders
    for _, order in ipairs(here.on_order or {}) do
      if order.resource == "Goods" then
        position = position + order.amount
      end
    end
    if memory.after ~= nil then
      local used = memory.after - position
      if memory.forecast == nil then
        memory.forecast = used
      else
        memory.forecast = ALPHA * used + (1 - ALPHA) * memory.forecast
      end
      ctx.record("demand", used / 1000)
      ctx.record("forecast", memory.forecast / 1000)
    end
    local target = math.floor((memory.forecast or 0) * COVER + SAFETY + 0.5)
    local amount = 0
    if target > position then
      amount = target - position
      ctx.order("Goods", amount)
    end
    memory.after = position + amount
  end,
}
```

Run it, then set `SAFETY` to `17000` and to `18000`, which are 17 and 18 units, and run again.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Observed demand.** The policy infers the demand from the fall in its inventory position, which
  includes backorders, so it observes demand that found no stock. With lost sales it would observe only
  the sales, and its forecasts would be too low (Axsäter, 2015, p. 29).
- **Daily steps.** The demand rate changes once a day, by the trend. The model's periods are these days.
- **Seasons.** The template's season raises the demand in the first half of each season and lowers it in
  the second.

## Exercises

1. Let $\alpha = 0.5$ in <Ref to="ex-forecast-shortfall" />. Compute the forecast error to which
   exponential smoothing converges, and the shortfall over the protection interval.

<Answer>

The error converges to $b / \alpha = 2$ units. The shortfall is
$3 \times 1 \times 0.5 / 0.5 + 1 \times 3 \times 4 / 2 = 3 + 6 = 9$ units.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-faster-smoothing" />

</Answer>

2. The demand grows by 2 units per period, and a station forecasts with a moving average over 9
   periods. Compute the forecast error, and the smoothing constant with the same lag.

<Answer>

By <Ref to="prop-forecast-lag" /> the error is $b\,(M + 1)/2 = 2 \times 10 / 2 = 10$ units. The matching
constant satisfies $2/\alpha - 1 = 9$, so $\alpha = 0.2$, which gives $b / \alpha = 10$.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-ma-lag" />

</Answer>

3. Under the level model, compute the standard deviation of the forecast error, as a multiple of
   $\sigma$, for exponential smoothing with $\alpha = 0.2$ and with $\alpha = 0.5$.

<Answer>

By <Ref to="prop-forecast-variance" /> the variance of the error is
$\sigma^2\,(1 + \alpha/(2 - \alpha)) = 2\sigma^2 / (2 - \alpha)$. For $\alpha = 0.2$ the standard deviation
is $\sqrt{2/1.8}\,\sigma = 1.054\,\sigma$, the same as for a moving average over 9 periods. For
$\alpha = 0.5$ it is $\sqrt{2/1.5}\,\sigma = 1.155\,\sigma$.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-error-variance" />

</Answer>

4. In the reference policy, set `ALPHA = 0.5`, and run it with `SAFETY = 8000` and with
   `SAFETY = 9000`. Compare the result with exercise 1.

<Answer>

With a safety stock of 8 units, demand is backordered on every day from 40 to 59. With 9 units it is
backordered on none of them, as the shortfall of 9 units predicts.

<GameNote>

A colony's demand grows as the colony grows. A dispatch rule that supplies last week's consumption falls
behind a growing colony by a constant amount, as exponential smoothing does behind a trend. A rule that
also estimates how fast a station's consumption is rising can follow the growth, as Holt's method does.

</GameNote>
