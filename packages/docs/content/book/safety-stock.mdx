## What you will learn

- To define the cycle service level and the fill rate, and to compute both for a base-stock level.
- To prove the formula for the variance of demand over an interval of random length.
- To compute the safety stock that a service target requires, exactly and by the normal approximation.
- To measure how a variable lead time and a higher target increase the safety stock.

## The problem

Chapter 6 chose a base-stock level by weighing a holding cost against a backorder cost. The backorder
cost is often unknown. A pharmacy or a store of spare parts is instead given a service target: a bound
on how often it may run short, or a share of demand that it must meet from stock.

A target is met by keeping more stock than the mean demand before the next delivery. The excess is the
safety stock. This chapter defines two measures of service and computes each for a given base-stock
level (Axsäter, 2015, pp. 79–81; Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 105–113). It then finds the safety stock
that a target requires, and shows how that stock depends on the variability of the demand and of the
lead time.

## The model

### The Shop with a service target

<Assumption id="asm-shop-service" name="The Shop with a service target">

The Shop of <Ref to="asm-shop-lead-time" /> is changed as follows.

1. Each order's lead time is drawn independently from a given distribution. Any two lead times differ by
   less than the review period $R$, so orders arrive in the sequence in which they were placed.
2. No backorder cost is given. The Shop uses a base-stock policy with level $S$, chosen to meet a
   service target.

</Assumption>

The chapter's examples and exercises use the following values, with time in days.

| Parameter | Symbol | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand rate | $\lambda$ | 10 units a day |
| Review period | $R$ | 7 days |
| Lead time | $L$ | 1 day or 3 days, each with probability $1/2$ |

### Two measures of service

<Definition id="def-service" name="Cycle service level and fill rate">

An **order cycle** is the interval between the arrivals of two successive orders. The **cycle service
level** is the probability that a cycle ends with no backorders. The **fill rate** is the long-run
fraction of demand that is met from stock on arrival, without waiting.

</Definition>

Snyder and Shen (2019, pp. 105–113) call these the type-1 and type-2 service levels. A cycle that ends
one unit short counts fully against the first measure and removes one unit from the second, so the fill
rate of a given level is usually much the higher.

<Proposition id="prop-csl" name="Cycle service level of a base-stock level">

Let $T = R + L$, where $L$ is a lead time, and let $D_T$ be the demand over an interval of length $T$.
Under a base-stock policy with level $S$, the cycle service level is

$$
\mathrm{CSL}(S) = P(D_T \le S) = \sum_{\ell} P(L = \ell)\, F_{R + \ell}(S),
$$

where $F_u$ is the distribution function of the demand over an interval of length $u$.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

Let a review occur at time $t$, and let the order placed at the next review, at $t + R$, have lead time
$L$. The cycle that this order ends finishes at $t + R + L$. By the first property of
<Ref to="asm-shop-service" />, every order placed at or before $t$ has arrived by then, and no order
placed after $t$ has. As in <Ref to="lem-level-after-lead-time" />, the inventory level just before the
arrival is therefore $S - D(t, t + R + L]$. The cycle ends with no backorders exactly when that demand
is at most $S$. The lead time is independent of the demand, so conditioning on its value $\ell$ gives the
sum.

</Proof>

<Definition id="def-safety-stock" name="Safety stock">

The **safety stock** of a base-stock level $S$ is $S - E[D_T]$, the excess of the level over the mean
demand before the next delivery.

</Definition>

<WorkedExample id="ex-csl">

For the Shop, $T$ is 8 or 10 days, so $D_T$ is Poisson with mean 80 or 100, each with probability
$1/2$, and $E[D_T] = 90$. The cycle service level is
$\mathrm{CSL}(S) = \tfrac12 F_8(S) + \tfrac12 F_{10}(S)$. Then $\mathrm{CSL}(112) < 0.95$ and
$\mathrm{CSL}(113) = 0.9546$, so a target of 0.95 requires $S = 113$ and a safety stock of 23 units.

</WorkedExample>

### Demand over a random interval

The safety stock depends on the variability of $D_T$, which has two sources: the demand varies, and so
does the length of the interval.

<Proposition id="prop-random-interval" name="Demand over a random interval">

Let demand be a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, and let $T \ge 0$ be a random variable independent
of it. Then

$$
E[D_T] = \lambda\,E[T], \qquad \operatorname{Var}(D_T) = \lambda\,E[T] + \lambda^2 \operatorname{Var}(T) .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

Given $T$, the demand $D_T$ is Poisson with mean and variance $\lambda T$. Taking the expectation of
the conditional mean gives $E[D_T] = \lambda E[T]$. The variance of a random variable is the mean of its
conditional variance plus the variance of its conditional mean:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(D_T) = E\big[\operatorname{Var}(D_T \mid T)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(E[D_T \mid T]\big)
= E[\lambda T] + \operatorname{Var}(\lambda T) .
$$

</Proof>

The first term is the variance that the demand contributes over an interval of the mean length. The
second is the contribution of the interval. It grows with the square of the demand rate, so a variable
lead time matters most where demand is high.

<WorkedExample id="ex-random-interval">

For the Shop $E[T] = 9$ and $\operatorname{Var}(T) = 1$, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(D_T) = 10 \times 9 + 10^2 \times 1 = 190, \qquad \sqrt{190} \approx 13.78 .
$$

With a fixed lead time of 2 days, which has the same mean, the variance is 90 and the standard deviation
is 9.49. The variation of one day in the lead time more than doubles the variance of the demand.

</WorkedExample>

### The normal approximation

<Proposition id="prop-safety-stock-normal" name="Safety stock by the normal approximation">

Let $D_T$ be approximated by a normal random variable with mean $E[D_T]$ and standard deviation
$\sigma_T = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(D_T)}$, and let $z_\alpha$ satisfy $\Phi(z_\alpha) = \alpha$. Then a
cycle service level of $\alpha$ requires a safety stock of

$$
S - E[D_T] = z_\alpha\,\sigma_T .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

$P(D_T \le S) = \Phi\big((S - E[D_T]) / \sigma_T\big)$, which equals $\alpha$ when
$(S - E[D_T]) / \sigma_T = z_\alpha$.

</Proof>

<WorkedExample id="ex-safety-stock">

For a target of 0.95, $z_{0.95} = 1.645$ and the safety stock is $1.645 \times 13.78 = 22.67$ units,
against the exact 23. With the fixed lead time of 2 days it is $1.645 \times 9.49 = 15.60$ units. The
exact level for the fixed lead time is 106, so the variability of the lead time costs 7 units of stock.

The safety stock rises steeply as the target approaches 1.

| Target $\alpha$ | 0.90 | 0.95 | 0.99 | 0.999 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $z_\alpha$ | 1.282 | 1.645 | 2.326 | 3.090 |
| Safety stock, normal approximation | 17.66 | 22.67 | 32.07 | 42.60 |
| Safety stock, exact | 18 | 23 | 31 | 40 |

</WorkedExample>

<Check refs="
  maxima:normal-approximation
  maxima:service-table
  maxima:fixed-lead-level
" />

The distribution of $D_T$ for the Shop is a mixture of two Poisson distributions. The approximation is
within one unit of the exact safety stock up to a target of 0.99, and overstates it by 2.6 units at
0.999.

### The fill rate

<Proposition id="prop-fill-rate" name="Fill rate of a base-stock level">

Suppose the backorders that remain just after an order arrives are negligible. Then the fill rate of a
base-stock level $S$ is

$$
\beta(S) = 1 - \frac{E\big[(D_T - S)^+\big]}{\lambda R} .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

By the proof of <Ref to="prop-csl" />, the backorders at the end of a cycle are $(D_T - S)^+$. If none
remained at its start, these are the units of demand that had to wait during the cycle. The mean demand
in a cycle is $\lambda R$, and the long-run fraction that waits is the ratio of the two means.

</Proof>

Under the normal approximation the expected backorders are $\sigma_T\,G(z)$ with
$z = (S - E[D_T]) / \sigma_T$, by <Ref to="prop-normal-demand" />.

<WorkedExample id="ex-fill-rate">

For the Shop with $S = 113$, the expected backorders at the end of a cycle are
$\tfrac12 E[(D_8 - 113)^+] + \tfrac12 E[(D_{10} - 113)^+] = 0.246$ units, and the demand in a cycle has
mean 70. Hence $\beta(113) = 1 - 0.246/70 = 0.9965$. The normal approximation gives
$13.78 \times G(1.669) = 0.272$ units and a fill rate of 0.9961.

A level that lets one cycle in twenty end with backorders therefore meets 99.65% of the demand from
stock. A statement of a service target must say which measure it means.

</WorkedExample>

## In the simulator

The `classic.safety_stock` template is the Shop of <Ref to="asm-shop-service" />, run for 10 weeks. The
supplier draws a lead time for each order. The template rejects lead times that differ by a review period
or more, which the first property of the assumption excludes.

<Scenario template="classic.safety_stock" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.safety_stock {
  lead_time = discrete { { days(1), 1 }, { days(3), 1 } },
}
```

<Check refs="

  test:rejects lead times that differ by a review period or more
" />

The reference policy is the base-stock policy with the level that a target of 0.95 requires:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.safety_stock
return ops.policy {
  review = { target = ops.order_up_to { level = 113000 } },
}
```

The simulator reports backorders and costs, and does not yet report the two service measures. The values
below are computed from the runs by a test, which counts a cycle as served when no demand is backordered
in the minute before a delivery, and omits the first cycle of each run. To see the effect in the
workbench, compare levels 113 and 106 in **Batch** and read the metrics **Backorders (average)** and
**Backorders (peak)**.

## Case study: storm shock

The `storm-shock` starter is the two-station line with a mine that produces 40 units a day and a dome
that consumes 24. A dust storm begins after three days. For one day it stops the mine. Half a day after
it begins, repairs raise the dome's consumption to two and a half times its normal rate, 60 units a day,
for a day and a half.

<Scenario starter="storm-shock" />

On a line, the stock at the demand station just after a visit corresponds to the base-stock level, and
its excess over the consumption between visits is the safety stock. The train's round trip takes
between $8\tfrac13$ and $9\tfrac13$ hours, in which the dome consumes 8 to 9 units. Under balancing the
dome has about 10 units when the storm begins, a cover of 10 hours at the normal rate and of 4 hours at
the raised rate. Over 50 replications its station runs out about 17 hours into the storm and is empty
for about 24 hours in all, into the day after the storm, and about 53 units of demand are unmet.

<Check ref="test:on storm-shock over 50 replications the balancing baseline holds about 10 units at the dome when the storm begins, the dome runs dry about 17 hours in and stands empty for about 24 hours, into the day after the storm, and about 53 units go unmet" />

The storm removes 40 units of production and adds 54 units of demand, a total of 94 units against a
station capacity of 30. No stock at the dome can cover it. The stock determines when the shortage begins
and how long it lasts. The mine resumes one day into the storm, and a dome that still has stock when
deliveries resume passes through with little or no shortage.

<Check ref="maxima:storm-shock" />

Drain-and-fill keeps the dome's stock as high as the line allows:

```lua runnable scenario=storm-shock fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual { Mine = "supply", Dome = "demand" },
  target = { supply = ops.drain {}, demand = ops.fill {} },
}
```

Between visits the dome's stock then falls from 30 to about 21, and it is about 23 units when the storm
begins, a cover of 9 hours at the raised rate. The station runs out only late on the fifth day, for less
than two hours, and about 4 units are unmet. An $(s, S)$ target that lets the dome fall to 20 before
refilling it keeps 19 units on average and leaves about 12 units unmet:

```lua runnable scenario=storm-shock
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual { Mine = "supply", Dome = "demand" },
  target = {
    supply = ops.drain {},
    demand = ops.min_max { min = 20000, max = 30000 },
  },
}
```

<Check ref="test:on storm-shock over 50 replications keeping the dome full holds about 23 units there when the storm begins, the dome stands empty for under 2 hours, none of it after the storm, and about 4 units go unmet, while a min-max target of 20 to 30 holds 19 on average and leaves about 12" />

The safety stock of this chapter is sized for the ordinary variation of demand and lead time. A shock of
94 units lies outside that variation, and the calculation of <Ref to="prop-safety-stock-normal" /> does
not apply to it.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Service measures.** The simulator has no metric for the cycle service level or the fill rate. The
  test described above computes them from the recorded backorders.
- **Poisson demand.** The simulator draws Poisson demand in sub-steps of a minute, as chapter 3
  describes, so its variance is slightly below the model's.
- **Backorders.** The results assume that unmet demand waits. When it is lost, a station that runs out
  also fails to record how much demand there was, and the analysis changes.

## Exercises

Each exercise uses the Shop's values in the table under
[The Shop with a service target](#the-shop-with-a-service-target), except where it states otherwise.

1. Find the base-stock level and the safety stock that a cycle service level of 0.99 requires, exactly
   and by the normal approximation.

<Answer>

Exactly, $\mathrm{CSL}(121) = 0.9910$ is the first value of at least 0.99, so $S = 121$ and the safety
stock is 31 units. By <Ref to="prop-safety-stock-normal" />, $z_{0.99} = 2.326$ and the safety stock is
$2.326 \times 13.78 = 32.07$ units.

<Check refs="
  maxima:higher-target
  maxima:exercise-normal-99
" />

</Answer>

2. Let the Shop review twice a week, so that $R = 3.5$ days. Find the level that a cycle service level
   of 0.95 requires.

<Answer>

Now $T$ is 4.5 or 6.5 days and $D_T$ has mean 45 or 65. The smallest level with
$\tfrac12 F_{4.5}(S) + \tfrac12 F_{6.5}(S) \ge 0.95$ is $S = 75$. A shorter review period shortens the
protection interval, and both the mean demand over it and the safety stock fall.

<Check ref="maxima:twice-weekly" />

</Answer>

3. Compute the fill rate of the level $S = 106$.

<Answer>

The expected backorders at the end of a cycle are $E[(D_T - 106)^+] = 0.862$ units, so
$\beta(106) = 1 - 0.862/70 = 0.9877$. The level's cycle service level is 0.8715.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-fill-rate" />

</Answer>

4. With `level = 121000` in the reference policy, the test of the simulator section finds over 200
   replications that 0.989 of 1,800 cycles end without backorders. Decide whether this result is
   consistent with a cycle service level of 0.9910.

<Answer>

By <Ref to="prop-proportion" />, a proportion of 1,800 independent cycles with $\theta = 0.991$ has a
standard error of $\sqrt{0.991 \times 0.009 / 1800} = 0.0022$. The difference of 0.002 is about one
standard error, so the result is consistent with the model.

<Check ref="test:on classic.safety_stock over 200 replications level 121 meets a 0.99 cycle service level" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

A train's round trip corresponds to the lead time, and it varies: a train that takes longer to load or
unload a large delivery returns later. By <Ref to="prop-random-interval" />, the more the interval
between visits varies, the more stock a station needs to avoid running out, even when its consumers use
the resource at a constant rate.

</GameNote>
