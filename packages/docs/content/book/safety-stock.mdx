## What you will learn

- To state a service target as a cycle service level or a fill rate, and to see why the
  two can differ a great deal.
- To set an order-up-to level that meets a cycle service level when demand is random and
  the lead time varies.
- To estimate safety stock with the normal approximation, and to see how much a variable
  lead time adds.

## The problem

Chapter 6 set a base-stock level from costs: what a backorder costs against what a unit
held costs. Often nobody can say what a backorder costs. A hospital pharmacy, a
maintenance store or a spare-parts depot is instead given a target: run out in no more
than one cycle in twenty, say, or meet 99% of demand straight from the shelf.

A target like that is met with safety stock, stock held above what demand is expected to
be before the next delivery. More variability, in demand or in how long the supplier
takes, needs more of it, and the stock needed climbs steeply as the target approaches
100% (Axsäter, 2015, pp. 79–81, 86–87; Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 105–113).

## The model

### Two ways to measure service

With periodic review and an order-up-to level $S$, each order starts a cycle that ends
when the next order arrives. Two common measures of service are:

- **Cycle service level:** the chance that a cycle ends with no customer waiting.
- **Fill rate:** the share of demand met straight from stock.

A cycle that runs short by one unit fails the first measure completely but barely dents
the second, so for the same level the fill rate is usually much higher.

### Cycle service level with a variable lead time

An order placed at a review raises the inventory position to $S$. Everything ordered
until then will have arrived by the time the next order does, and nothing ordered later
will have. So the cycle ends with no one waiting exactly when demand over the review
period $R$ and the next order's lead time $L$ is at most $S$. With Poisson demand at rate
$\lambda$ and lead times $\ell$ drawn with chances $p_\ell$,

$$
\text{CSL}(S) = \sum_{\ell} p_\ell \, P\big(\text{Poisson}(\lambda(R + \ell)) \le S\big).
$$

This needs the orders not to overtake each other, which holds when lead times differ by
less than a review period.

Take demand of 10 a day, weekly reviews, and a lead time of 1 or 3 days with equal
chance. Demand over a cycle has mean 80 or 100, 90 on average.

<Check ref="maxima:cycle-means" />

The smallest level with a cycle service level of at least 0.95 is 113, reaching 0.9546.
Its safety stock is $113 - 90 = 23$ units.

<Check ref="maxima:service-level" />

### The normal approximation

Demand over an interval of random length $T$ has variance

$$
\operatorname{Var}(D_T) = \lambda\,\mathbb{E}[T] + \lambda^2 \operatorname{Var}(T).
$$

The first term is the randomness of demand, the second the randomness of the interval.
Here $\mathbb{E}[T] = 9$ days and $\operatorname{Var}(T) = 1$, so the variance is
$90 + 100 = 190$ and the standard deviation about 13.78.

<Check ref="maxima:interval-variance" />

Approximating demand as normal, safety stock for a 95% cycle service level is
$z_{0.95}\,\sigma$, with $z_{0.95} \approx 1.645$:

$$
1.645 \times \sqrt{190} \approx 22.67,
$$

<Check ref="maxima:normal-approximation" />

close to the exact 23. With a fixed lead time of 2 days, the same average, the variance
is only 90 and the approximate safety stock 15.60. The exact level for a fixed lead time
is 106, so the lead time's variability alone costs 7 units of stock.

<Check ref="maxima:fixed-lead-level" />

### Fill rate

Approximate the fill rate by the expected shortfall at the end of a cycle, divided by
the demand in a cycle:

$$
\beta \approx 1 - \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(D_T - S)^+\right]}{\lambda R}.
$$

At a level of 113 the expected shortfall is about 0.246 units against a week's demand of
70, a fill rate of about 0.996. A level that lets one cycle in twenty end short still meets
over 99% of demand from the shelf.

<Check ref="maxima:fill-rate" />

## In the simulator

The `classic.safety_stock` template has one store with Poisson demand, backorders, weekly
reviews and a lead time that the supplier draws for each order. Its defaults are the
worked case, over 10 weeks.

<Scenario template="classic.safety_stock" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.safety_stock {
  lead_time = discrete { { days(1), 1 }, { days(3), 1 } },
}
```

<Check ref="test:describes a store with Poisson demand, backorders and lead times of one and three days with equal weights" />

The reference policy orders up to the level that meets the target cycle service level,
113 for a target of 0.95:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.safety_stock
return ops.policy {
  review = { target = ops.order_up_to { level = 113000 } },
}
```

A test runs it for 400 seeds, and in each run counts the cycles that end with no one
waiting, just before each delivery after the first. The analytic 0.9546 lies inside the
99% confidence interval of the simulated share.

<Check ref="test:meets the analytic cycle service level of its reference level over 400 seeds" />

The template refuses lead times that differ by a review period or more, because orders
could then overtake each other and the formula above would no longer hold.

<Check ref="test:rejects lead times that differ by a review period or more" />

## Case study: storm shock

The `storm-shock` starter is chapter 1's line with a smaller mine, 40 units a day against
the dome's 24, and a dust storm that arrives after three days. For one day the storm stops
the mine: a supply shock. Half a day after it starts, repairs raise the dome's use to two
and a half times normal, 60 units a day, for a day and a half: a demand shock. Both are
states of the world, in which rates change while they last, and the timeline shows each as
a band.

<Scenario starter="storm-shock" />

On a line, safety stock is what the demand station holds between visits. The train's
round trip takes between eight and a third and nine and a third hours, so the dome uses
eight to nine units between one visit and the next. Under balancing it holds about 10
units when the storm begins, ten hours of normal use and four of the surge. Over seeds 1
to 50 its station runs dry about 17 hours into the storm, stands empty for about 24
hours in all, is still empty into the day after the storm has passed, and about 53 units
of demand go unmet.

<Check ref="test:on storm-shock over seeds 1 to 50 the balancing baseline holds about 10 units at the dome when the storm begins, the dome runs dry about 17 hours in and stands empty for about 24 hours, into the day after the storm, and about 53 units go unmet" />

The shock is larger than any buffer a 30-unit station can hold. The storm takes away 40
units of the mine's output and adds 54 units to the dome's demand, 94 units against a
station that holds 30. No safety stock rides it out. What the buffer decides is when the
shortage starts and how long it lasts: the mine comes back a day into the storm and
deliveries resume, and a dome still holding stock when they do hardly notices.

<Check ref="maxima:storm-shock" />

So keep the dome full, with the roles policy of chapter 1:

```lua runnable scenario=storm-shock fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual { Mine = "supply", Dome = "demand" },
  target = { supply = ops.drain {}, demand = ops.fill {} },
}
```

Between visits the dome now falls from 30 to about 21, and it holds about 23 units when
the storm begins, nine hours of the surge. That reaches the mine's return: the station
runs dry only late on the fifth day, for under two hours, with nothing after the storm,
and about 4 units go unmet. A min–max target that lets the dome fall to 20 before
refilling it holds less on average, 19 units, and leaves about 12 unmet. The buffer it
gives up between refills is cover the storm needed.

```lua runnable scenario=storm-shock
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual { Mine = "supply", Dome = "demand" },
  target = {
    supply = ops.drain {},
    demand = ops.min_max { min = 20000, max = 30000 },
  },
}
```

<Check ref="test:on storm-shock over seeds 1 to 50 keeping the dome full holds about 23 units there when the storm begins, the dome stands empty for under 2 hours, none of it after the storm, and about 4 units go unmet, while a min-max target of 20 to 30 holds 19 on average and leaves about 12" />

The chapter's method sizes safety stock for ordinary variability, and the few units that
the dome's wandering use needs over a nine-hour visit are small beside a shock like this.
A disruption is not variability. Part III returns to shocks of this size, and to what a
line can do about them beyond holding a station full.

## Where the simulator differs

- **No service level metric yet.** The simulator reports backorders and costs, not the
  cycle service level. The test above measures it from the run, counting a cycle as met
  when no demand is waiting in the minute before a delivery, and leaves out the first
  cycle, which starts from empty stock.
- **Binomial arrivals.** Poisson demand is drawn in small sub-steps within each minute,
  as chapter 3 describes, so its variance is very slightly lower than the model's.
- **Backorders only.** With lost sales, stock that runs out also hides how much demand
  there was, and the analysis changes.

## Exercises

1. The target rises to 0.99. What level does the worked case need?

<Answer>

2. Reviews happen twice a week instead, every 3.5 days. What level meets a 0.95 target?

<Answer>

Demand over a cycle now has mean 45 or 65, and the level falls to 75. More frequent
reviews shorten the protection interval, and the stock needed falls with it.

<Check ref="maxima:twice-weekly" />

</Answer>

3. Using the normal approximation, how much safety stock does a fixed lead time of 2 days
   need for a 95% cycle service level?

<Answer>

About $1.645 \times \sqrt{90} \approx 15.60$ units, against about 22.67 when the lead time
varies between 1 and 3 days.

<Check ref="maxima:normal-approximation" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

A train's round trip plays the part of the lead time, and it varies: a train that stops
longer to load or unload a big delivery takes longer to return. The more a line's visit
interval varies, the more stock a station must hold to avoid running dry, even when its
consumers use resources at a perfectly steady rate.

</GameNote>
