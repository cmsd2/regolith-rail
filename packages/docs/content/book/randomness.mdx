## What you will learn

- To model random demand, and to compute the mean and variance of the demand over an interval.
- To estimate the mean of a simulated output from independent replications, and to compute a confidence
  interval for it.
- To determine the number of replications that a required precision needs.
- To compare two policies with common random numbers, and to determine when pairing reduces the variance
  of the comparison.

## The problem

Chapters 1 and 2 took demand to be constant. The starter scenarios draw demand at random, and the output
of a run, such as its unmet demand, then depends on the random numbers drawn. A single run is one
observation of a random variable.

A simulation is therefore a statistical experiment, and its output requires the methods of statistical
inference (Taha, 2017, p. 728). This chapter models random demand, estimates the mean of an output from
independent replications of a run, and quantifies the error of the estimate. It then compares two policies
through the difference of their outputs, and shows how running both on the same random numbers changes the
variance of that difference.

The chapter assumes the Poisson and normal distributions, the mean and variance of a sum of independent
random variables, the central limit theorem and Student's $t$ distribution. It recalls each where it is
used.

## The model

### Random demand

<Definition id="def-poisson-process" name="Poisson process">

Let $A(t)$ be the number of units demanded in $[0, t]$. The demand is a **Poisson process** with rate
$\lambda$ if $A(0) = 0$, the numbers demanded in disjoint intervals are independent, and the number
demanded in any interval of length $t$ has the Poisson distribution with mean $\lambda t$:

$$
P\big(A(t) = k\big) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^k}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

</Definition>

The mean and the variance of $A(t)$ both equal $\lambda t$.

<Check ref="maxima:poisson-mean-variance" />

<WorkedExample id="ex-shortage" name="Shortage between deliveries">

A station has $x$ units in stock when a vehicle departs, and the next delivery arrives after a time $T$.
Demand is a Poisson process with $\lambda T = 10$. The station runs short before the delivery if
$A(T) > x$, which has probability

$$
P\big(A(T) > x\big) = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{x} \frac{e^{-10}\, 10^k}{k!} .
$$

| Stock $x$ | 10 | 12 | 15 | 18 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $P(A(T) > x)$ | 0.4170 | 0.2084 | 0.0487 | 0.0072 |

A stock equal to the mean demand leaves a probability of shortage above 0.4. Chapter 7 chooses the stock
that makes this probability acceptably small.

</WorkedExample>

<Check ref="maxima:shortage-probability" />

<WorkedExample id="ex-line-demand" name="Demand on the two-station line">

On the two-station line of <Ref to="asm-line" />, let the dome's consumption rate be constant within each
two-hour block and equal to $u\,(1 + U_j)$ in block $j$, where the $U_j$ are independent and uniformly
distributed on $[-0.2, 0.2]$. Then $E[U_j] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(U_j) = 0.4^2 / 12 = 1/75$. With
$u = 3/2$ units an hour, the demand in block $j$ is $3\,(1 + U_j)$ units. A run of 10 days has 120 blocks,
and its total demand $D = \sum_{j=1}^{120} 3\,(1 + U_j)$ has

$$
E[D] = 120 \times 3 = 360, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(D) = 120 \times 9 \times \frac{1}{75} = 14.4, \qquad
\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(D)} \approx 3.79 .
$$

As a sum of 120 independent terms, $D$ is approximately normal by the central limit theorem.

</WorkedExample>

<Check ref="maxima:line-demand" />

### Replications

Fix a scenario and a policy, and choose an output of a run, such as the unmet demand over the run. The
value $X$ of the output depends on the random numbers drawn during the run, so $X$ is a random variable. Write
$\mu_X$ for its mean and $\sigma_X^2$ for its variance. The aim is to estimate $\mu_X$.

<Definition id="def-replication" name="Replications">

A **replication** is a run whose random numbers are drawn independently of those of every other run.
The outputs $X_1, \dots, X_n$ of $n$ replications are independent and have the distribution of $X$.
Their **sample mean** and **sample variance** are

$$
\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i, \qquad S_n^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(X_i - \bar X_n\big)^2 .
$$

</Definition>

<Proposition id="prop-sample-mean" name="Mean and variance of the sample mean">

$$
E\big[\bar X_n\big] = \mu_X, \qquad \operatorname{Var}\big(\bar X_n\big) = \frac{\sigma_X^2}{n} .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

The mean of a sum is the sum of the means, so $E[\bar X_n] = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i E[X_i] = \mu_X$. The
replications are independent, so the variance of their sum is the sum of their variances, $n \sigma_X^2$.
Dividing the sum by $n$ divides its variance by $n^2$.

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:sample-statistics
  maxima:sample-mean
" />

The proposition is Theorem 3.17 of Wasserman (2004, p. 52). It makes $\bar X_n$ an **unbiased** estimator
of $\mu_X$, and its standard deviation $\sigma_X / \sqrt{n}$ is the **standard error** of the estimate.
The independence of the replications is what the proof uses. Observations taken from successive intervals
of a single long run are not independent, and the variance formula does not apply to them (Taha, 2017,
pp. 728–729).

<Corollary id="cor-root-n">

The standard error is proportional to $1 / \sqrt{n}$. Halving it requires four times as many
replications.

</Corollary>

<Check ref="maxima:standard-error" />

<Definition id="def-confidence-interval" name="Confidence interval for a mean">

By the central limit theorem, $\bar X_n$ is approximately normal with mean $\mu_X$ and standard deviation
$\sigma_X / \sqrt{n}$ when $n$ is large. With $S_n$ in place of the unknown $\sigma_X$, the interval

$$
\bar X_n \pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\, \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

is an approximate **95% confidence interval** for $\mu_X$, where $t_{n-1,\,0.975}$ is the 0.975 quantile
of Student's $t$ distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom. Its **half-width** is
$t_{n-1,\,0.975}\, S_n / \sqrt{n}$.

</Definition>

In repeated experiments about 95% of such intervals contain $\mu_X$ (Wasserman, 2004, pp. 77, 92). The
interval is exact when $X$ is normal. The quantile is 2.262 for $n = 10$, 2.064 for $n = 25$, 1.984 for
$n = 100$ and 1.966 for $n = 400$, and it tends to the normal quantile 1.960.

<Check ref="python:t-critical" />

<WorkedExample id="ex-known-mean" name="A mean that is known">

Let $X$ be the total demand $D$ of <Ref to="ex-line-demand" />, so that $\mu_X = 360$ and
$\sigma_X \approx 3.79$ are known. The standard error of $\bar X_n$ and the half-width of the interval,
with $\sigma_X$ in place of $S_n$, are as follows.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Standard error $\sigma_X / \sqrt{n}$ | 0.759 | 0.379 | 0.190 |
| Half-width | 1.57 | 0.75 | 0.37 |

An output with a known mean tests the method. The interval computed from simulated replications should
contain 360, and its half-width should be close to the value above. The simulator section carries out the

</WorkedExample>

<Corollary id="cor-replications" name="Replications for a given half-width">

For large $n$ the half-width is approximately $z\,\sigma_X / \sqrt{n}$ with $z = 1.96$. A half-width of at
most $\delta$ therefore requires approximately

$$
n \ \ge\ \left(\frac{z\,\sigma_X}{\delta}\right)^2
$$

replications.

</Corollary>

<WorkedExample id="ex-pilot" name="A mean that is not known">

Let $X$ be the unmet demand of the two-station line under balancing over a run of 10 days, with the
demand of <Ref to="ex-line-demand" />. Its mean is not known. A batch of 100 replications gives
$\bar X_{100} = 27.97$ and $S_{100} = 3.00$ units. The standard error is $3.00 / \sqrt{100} = 0.30$, and the
95% confidence interval is $27.97 \pm 1.984 \times 0.30$, from 27.37 to 28.57.

The batch also serves as a pilot for a more precise estimate. With $S_{100}$ as an estimate of $\sigma_X$,
a half-width of 0.25 requires about $(1.96 \times 3.00 / 0.25)^2 \approx 553$ replications.

</WorkedExample>

### Estimating a probability

The probability of an event is the mean of its indicator, so the same method estimates it.

<Proposition id="prop-proportion" name="Estimating a probability">

Let $X = 1$ if an event occurs in a run and $X = 0$ otherwise, and let $\theta$ be the probability of the
event. Then $\mu_X = \theta$ and

$$
\sigma_X^2 = \theta\,(1 - \theta) \le \frac14, \qquad\text{so}\qquad
\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\big(\bar X_n\big)} = \sqrt{\frac{\theta\,(1 - \theta)}{n}} \le \frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}} .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

Since $X^2 = X$, both $E[X]$ and $E[X^2]$ equal $\theta$, and $\sigma_X^2 = \theta - \theta^2$. The
function $\theta\,(1 - \theta)$ has its maximum on $[0, 1]$ at $\theta = 1/2$, where it equals $1/4$. The
standard error follows from <Ref to="prop-sample-mean" />.

</Proof>

<WorkedExample id="ex-proportion">

For the shortage of <Ref to="ex-shortage" /> with $x = 12$, $\theta = 0.2084$. With 400 replications the
standard error of the estimated probability is $\sqrt{0.2084 \times 0.7916 / 400} = 0.0203$. A half-width
of 0.01 requires about $1.96^2 \times 0.2084 \times 0.7916 / 0.01^2 \approx 6337$ replications.

</WorkedExample>

### Comparing two policies

Let $X$ and $Y$ be the same output under two policies. The comparison estimates $\mu_X - \mu_Y$. Its
precision depends on how the pairs of runs are drawn.

<Proposition id="prop-difference" name="Variance of a difference">

Let $X$ and $Y$ have variances $\sigma_X^2$ and $\sigma_Y^2$ and correlation $\rho_{XY}$. Then

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X - Y) = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\rho_{XY}\,\sigma_X \sigma_Y .
$$

If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $\rho_{XY} = 0$ and the variance is $\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2$.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

$\operatorname{Var}(X - Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) - 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$, and
by the definition of the correlation $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \rho_{XY}\,\sigma_X \sigma_Y$.

</Proof>

<Definition id="def-crn" name="Common random numbers">

The method of **common random numbers** runs replication $i$ of both policies with the same random
numbers, so that both policies face the same disturbances. The pairs $(X_i, Y_i)$ are independent of one
another, and within a pair $X_i$ and $Y_i$ have correlation $\rho_{XY}$. The differences
$Z_i = X_i - Y_i$ have sample mean $\bar Z_n$ and sample standard deviation $S_Z$, and

$$
\bar Z_n \pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\, \frac{S_Z}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

is an approximate 95% confidence interval for $\mu_X - \mu_Y$.

</Definition>

Law (2015, ch. 11) treats the method among the techniques of variance reduction. The interval is that of
<Ref to="def-confidence-interval" /> applied to the differences, which are independent of one another
although $X_i$ and $Y_i$ are not.

<Corollary id="cor-crn">

Common random numbers give $\bar Z_n$ a smaller variance than independent replications of the two
policies if and only if $\rho_{XY} > 0$. If $\sigma_X = \sigma_Y$, the variance is multiplied by
$1 - \rho_{XY}$.

</Corollary>

<Proof>

By <Ref to="prop-difference" /> and <Ref to="prop-sample-mean" />, the variance of $\bar Z_n$ is
$(\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\rho_{XY}\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y)/n$ with common random numbers and
$(\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2)/n$ with independent replications. The first is smaller exactly when
$\rho_{XY} > 0$. With $\sigma_X = \sigma_Y = \sigma$ their ratio is
$2\sigma^2 (1 - \rho_{XY}) / 2\sigma^2 = 1 - \rho_{XY}$.

</Proof>

<WorkedExample id="ex-crn" name="Production lost at the mine">

Let $X$ and $Y$ be the production that the mine loses over a run of 10 days under balancing and under
drain-and-fill. In 100 replications with common random numbers the sample standard deviations are 0.92
and 3.59 units, and the sample correlation is 0.786. A replication with low demand leaves more production
undelivered under either policy, so the two outputs rise and fall together. With these values,

$$
\sqrt{0.92^2 + 3.59^2} = 3.71, \qquad
\sqrt{0.92^2 + 3.59^2 - 2 \times 0.786 \times 0.92 \times 3.59} = 2.92 .
$$

The paired differences have standard deviation 2.92, against 3.71 for independent replications. The
variance is smaller by the factor $2.92^2 / 3.71^2 = 0.62$, so the paired comparison attains a given
precision with 62% of the replications. The half-width of the interval for the difference in means is
0.58 with pairing and 0.74 without.

</WorkedExample>

<Remark id="rem-crn-negative">

Common random numbers increase the variance when $\rho_{XY} < 0$. On the same line the share of distance
that the train travels empty has a negative correlation between the two policies, and its paired
differences vary more than differences of independent replications would. The sign of the correlation
depends on how the two outputs respond to the same disturbances, and the replications themselves provide
an estimate of it.

</Remark>

### Runs of finite length

<Remark id="rem-transient">

A replication estimates the mean of an output over a run of a given length from a given initial state.
That mean depends on both. The initial period of a run, before the effect of the initial state has faded,
is its **transient** period, and each replication includes it (Taha, 2017, pp. 728–730). Chapter 2 gave an
instance: on the relay line the dome's mean stock was 23.00 units over 10 days and 23.28 over 20, against
23.56 in the steady state. An estimate of a steady-state mean requires long runs, or the removal of the
transient period from each run.

</Remark>

## In the simulator

Every run takes a seed. For a given scenario and policy, the same seed gives the same run. A batch takes
a base seed and a number of replications. Each replication draws its random numbers from its own stream, derived
from the base seed and the replication's number, so the replications are independent and a batch can be
repeated exactly. A comparison runs both policies on the same streams, which is the method of
<Ref to="def-crn" />.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Poisson demand.** Where a scenario specifies Poisson demand, the simulator divides each minute into
  at least eight sub-steps and gives each a small probability of an arrival, because exact Poisson
  sampling needs floating-point functions that differ between browsers. The count has the exact mean.
  Its variance is below the Poisson variance by the factor one minus the probability per sub-step, which
  is $1 - 1/2880$ at 4 arrivals a day.

  <Check refs="
    maxima:binomial-substeps
    test:gives Poisson arrivals their mean rate over many days
  " />
- **Critical values.** The batch view takes $t_{n-1,\,0.975}$ from a table and uses the entry for the
  nearest smaller number of replications, so its intervals are never narrower than the exact ones.

  <Check ref="test:uses conservative t critical values" />
- **Labels.** The batch view calls the number of replications **Seeds**. Each replication's stream
  depends only on the base seed and the replication's number, so adding replications leaves the earlier
  ones unchanged.

## Exercises

1. Demand is a Poisson process with a rate of 4 units a day. Compute the probability that no unit is
   demanded in a day, and the probability that at most two are.

<Answer>

$P(A(1) = 0) = e^{-4} \approx 0.0183$, and
$P(A(1) \le 2) = e^{-4}\,(1 + 4 + 8) = 13\,e^{-4} \approx 0.2381$.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-poisson" />

</Answer>

2. A batch of 100 replications gives a confidence interval with half-width 0.60. Estimate the number of
   replications that would give a half-width of 0.30, and of 0.25.

<Answer>

By <Ref to="cor-root-n" /> the half-width is proportional to $1/\sqrt{n}$, so halving it to 0.30 requires
about 400 replications. For 0.25, <Ref to="cor-replications" /> with $\sigma_X \approx 3.00$ gives
$(1.96 \times 3.00 / 0.25)^2 \approx 553.2$, so 554 replications.

<Check refs="
  maxima:standard-error
  maxima:replications-needed
" />

</Answer>

3. Two policies' outputs have equal variances and a correlation of 0.75 under common random numbers.
   Compare the number of replications that a paired comparison needs with the number that independent
   replications need for the same precision.

<Answer>

By <Ref to="cor-crn" /> the variance of the paired difference is $1 - 0.75 = 1/4$ of the variance with
independent replications. The variance of a sample mean is inversely proportional to $n$, so the paired
comparison needs a quarter of the replications.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-equal-variances" />

</Answer>

4. Repeat the comparison of the simulator section with 10 replications in place of 100. Decide whether
   the interval for the difference in unmet demand still excludes zero.

<Answer>

It does. Over the first 10 replications the difference has sample mean 28.42 and sample standard
deviation 2.55, so the half-width is $2.262 \times 2.55 / \sqrt{10} = 1.82$ and the interval runs from
26.6 to 30.2. A difference that is large relative to the standard deviation of a single replication needs
few replications. A small difference needs many.

<Check refs="

  test:on two-station over 10 replications the paired difference in unmet demand between the drain-and-fill rule and balancing is about 28 units with a 95% half-width under 2, so ten replications already leave out zero
" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

Colonies in the rail game do not consume resources at a constant rate, and the starter scenarios model
this with rates that vary from one two-hour block to the next. A single run of a line is one possible
history of the colony. A comparison of two dispatch rules needs a batch of replications and an interval
for the difference that excludes zero.

</GameNote>
