## What you will learn

- To describe random arrivals with the Poisson process, and to work out the mean and
  spread of demand over a stretch of time.
- To estimate an expected cost by averaging many simulated runs, and to say how precise
  that estimate is with a standard error and a confidence interval.
- To compare two policies fairly by running them on the same random numbers, and to see
  why that needs far fewer runs than comparing independent runs.

## The problem

Real operations are noisy. Customers arrive when they arrive, machines break when they
break, and the same policy run twice in the real world gives two different results. A
single run of a simulation is one draw from that noise. It can make a poor policy look
good, or a good one look poor.

The remedy is to treat a simulation as an experiment. Run it many times with different
random numbers, average the results, and measure how much the average could still move.
When comparing two policies, run both on the same random numbers so the comparison sees
their difference and not the luck of the draw (Law, 2015; Wasserman, 2004).

## The model

### Random arrivals

When arrivals are independent and happen at a steady average rate $\lambda$, the number
that occur in a time $t$ follows the Poisson distribution with mean $m = \lambda t$:

$$
P(N = k) = \frac{e^{-m} m^k}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
$$

Its mean and its variance are both $m$, so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{m}$. Demand
that averages 9 units over a stretch of time typically lands within 3 units of that.

<Check ref="maxima:poisson-mean-variance" />

The chance of no arrival at all in a gap of length $s$ is the Poisson chance of zero,

$$
P(\text{no arrival in } s) = e^{-\lambda s},
$$

so the gaps between arrivals are exponential, with mean $1/\lambda$.

<Check ref="maxima:exponential-gap" />

### Demand over a protection interval

Inventory decisions care about demand over a stretch of time: the lead time before an
order arrives, plus the time until the next chance to order. With 4 arrivals a day, a
lead time of 2 days and a review every 6 hours, demand over that stretch has mean
$4 \times 2.25 = 9$ and standard deviation 3. Chapter 6 builds on this.

<Check ref="maxima:protection-demand" />

### Estimating by simulation

Run a simulation $n$ times with different seeds and average the costs. The average
$\bar{X}$ estimates the expected cost. If one run's cost has standard deviation
$\sigma$, the average of $n$ independent runs has standard deviation

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},
$$

its standard error. Precision grows only with the square root of the effort: four times
as many runs halve the standard error.

<Check ref="maxima:standard-error" />

We do not know $\sigma$, so we estimate it with the sample standard deviation $s$, and
build a 95% confidence interval from Student's t distribution:

$$
\bar{X} \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}.
$$

<Check ref="python:t-critical" />

Here $t_{0.975,\,n-1}$ is the critical value of Student's t distribution with $n - 1$
degrees of freedom: 97.5% of the distribution lies below it, so 2.5% lies beyond it in
each tail and 95% between. Some books write it $t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}$, with $\alpha = 0.05$
the chance left outside. For 30 runs $t_{0.975,29} \approx 2.045$, and for 100 runs
$t_{0.975,99} \approx 1.984$, close to the normal value 1.96. With 100 runs, a mean of 7.9
and $s = 1.4$, the interval is $7.9 \pm 0.28$, from 7.62 to 8.18.

<Check ref="python:interval-example" />

The same idea estimates anything. The share of random points in a square that land
inside a quarter circle estimates $\pi/4$, and with 400,000 points its standard error, as
an estimate of $\pi$, is about 0.0026.

<Check ref="python:monte-carlo-pi" />

### Comparing on the same seeds

To compare policies A and B, look at the difference $D = X_B - X_A$. If the two are run
on unrelated random numbers, the variances simply add. If they are run on the same seed,
so both face the same arrivals, their costs move together with correlation $\rho$, and

$$
\operatorname{Var}(D) = \sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_A \sigma_B.
$$

With equal spreads and $\rho = 0.9$, the variance of the difference is a tenth of what
independent runs give, so the same precision needs a tenth of the runs. This technique is
called common random numbers. For two policies each with a standard deviation of 1.4,
the difference's standard deviation falls from 1.98 to 0.63.

<Check ref="maxima:paired-variance" />

The simulator makes randomness repeatable. Every run takes a seed, and the same scenario,
policy and seed give exactly the same run.

The `classic.reorder` template has one station replenished after a lead time. Open it,
then turn on **random** in its parameters for Poisson demand. This version has 4
arrivals a day, a lead time of 2 days and a review every 6 hours, as above:

```lua runnable script

  random = true,
  demand = 4,
  lead_time = days(2),
  review_period = hours(6),
  order_cost = 0,
  duration = days(30),

```

Its reference policy orders up to 13 units at each review: the smallest level that
demand over the protection interval stays at or below with probability at least
$10/11$, the ratio of the backorder cost to the backorder and holding costs together.

confidence interval.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Arrivals are a close approximation.** Exact Poisson sampling needs floating-point
  functions that differ between browsers, so the simulator splits each minute into at
  least eight sub-steps and gives each a small chance of an arrival. The mean is exact.
  The variance is lower than a Poisson count's by a factor of one minus the chance per
  sub-step, which at 4 arrivals a day is $1/2880$.

  <Check ref="maxima:binomial-substeps" />

- **A table of t values.** The batch view reads critical values from a table and uses
  the nearest smaller number of runs, so its intervals are never narrower than they
  should be.

  <Check ref="test:uses conservative t critical values" />

- **Batches repeat.** A batch's seeds are derived from a base seed and the number of
  runs, so running the same batch again gives the same results.

  <Check ref="test:derives batch seeds from base seed and count only" />


## Exercises

1. A batch of 100 runs gives a confidence interval of a certain width. How many runs
   would halve that width?

<Answer>

About 400. The width is proportional to $1/\sqrt{n}$, so halving it needs four times the
runs.

<Check ref="maxima:standard-error" />

</Answer>

2. Two policies' costs correlate at 0.75 when run on the same seeds, and have equal
   spreads. Compared with independent runs, how many runs does a paired comparison need
   for the same precision?

<Answer>

A quarter as many. The variance of the difference is $2\sigma^2(1 - 0.75)$, a quarter of
the $2\sigma^2$ that independent runs give.

<Check ref="maxima:paired-variance" />

</Answer>

3. Arrivals average 4 a day. What is the chance that a whole day passes with none?

<Answer>

$e^{-4} \approx 0.0183$, a little under 2%.

<Check ref="maxima:no-arrival-day" />

</Answer>
