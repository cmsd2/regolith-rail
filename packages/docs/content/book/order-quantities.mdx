## What you will learn

- To formulate the choice of an order quantity as the minimisation of an average cost with a fixed part
  for each order and a part for holding stock.
- To prove that an optimal plan orders equal quantities at the moments when the stock is zero, and to
  derive the economic order quantity.
- To compute the cost of ordering a quantity other than the optimal one.
- To extend the result to a lead time and to periodic review, where it takes the form of an $(s, S)$
  policy.

## The problem

Part I studied a line and the vehicle that serves it. Part II studies a single station that orders stock
from a supplier, and asks how much it should order and when. This chapter takes the simplest case: demand
is constant, and each order has a fixed cost that does not depend on its size.

A station that orders large quantities places few orders and has much stock. A station that orders small
quantities has little stock and places many orders. The economic order quantity, due to Harris in 1913,
minimises the sum of the two costs (Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 51–57; Axsäter, 2015, pp. 45–48). The
argument, in which one cost falls as a quantity grows while another rises, recurs throughout this part.

## The model

### The Shop

<Assumption id="asm-shop" name="The Shop">

A station, the Shop, sells one product and has the following properties.

1. Demand arrives at the constant rate $D$.
2. The Shop orders from a supplier whose stock is unlimited. Each order costs $K$, whatever its size.
3. Each unit in stock costs $h$ per unit time.
4. An order arrives at the moment it is placed.
5. All demand is met from stock.

</Assumption>

The chapter's examples and exercises use the following values, with time in days.

| Parameter | Symbol | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand rate | $D$ | 10 units a day |
| Cost of an order | $K$ | 20 |
| Cost of holding a unit | $h$ | 1 a day |

The price paid for each unit is omitted from the model. Every plan that meets all demand buys the same
number of units over a long period, so the price does not affect the comparison of plans (Snyder and Shen,
2019, p. 52).

<Definition id="def-plan" name="Plan and average cost">

A **plan** specifies the times at which the Shop places orders and the quantity of each. Let the plan
place $n(T)$ orders in $[0, T]$, and let $x(t)$ be the stock at time $t$. The cost of the plan over
$[0, T]$ is

$$
K\,n(T) + h \int_0^T x(t)\,dt .
$$

The **average cost** of the plan is the limit of this cost divided by $T$ as $T \to \infty$.

</Definition>

<Check ref="maxima:plan-cost" />

### The form of an optimal plan

Two lemmas reduce the choice of a plan to the choice of one number. Snyder and Shen (2019, pp. 51–52)
state both properties without proof.

<Lemma id="lem-zero-stock" name="Orders at zero stock">

A plan that places an order while the Shop has stock can be replaced by a plan of lower cost that places
every order at a moment when the stock is zero.

</Lemma>

<Proof>

Suppose the plan orders $q$ units at a time when $y > 0$ units are in stock. Delay that order until the
$y$ units are used up, a time $y / D$ later, or until the next order of the plan, whichever is sooner.
Demand is still met in the meantime. In the first case the plan places the same orders, and the $q$ units
are in stock for a time $y / D$ less, which saves $h\,q\,y / D$. In the second case the two orders
combine into one, which saves $K$, and the $q$ units are again in stock for less time. Repeating the step
for each such order gives a plan that orders only at zero stock.

</Proof>

<Lemma id="lem-equal-quantities" name="Equal quantities">

Consider the plans that order only at zero stock and place $n$ orders in an interval $[0, T]$ that begins
and ends with no stock. Among them, the plan with $n$ equal orders has the least cost.

</Lemma>

<Proof>

An order of $q$ units is used up in a time $q / D$, during which the mean stock is $q / 2$, so it incurs
a holding cost of $h\,q^2 / (2D)$. The quantities $q_1, \dots, q_n$ sum to the demand $DT$, and every such
plan pays $nK$ for its orders. With $\bar q = DT / n$,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i^2 = n\,\bar q^{\,2} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} (q_i - \bar q)^2 \ \ge\ n\,\bar q^{\,2},
$$

with equality only when every $q_i$ equals $\bar q$.

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:zero-stock-ordering
  maxima:equal-quantities
" />

It therefore suffices to consider the plans that order a fixed quantity $Q$ each time the stock reaches
zero.

### The economic order quantity

<Proposition id="prop-eoq-cost" name="Average cost of a fixed quantity">

The plan that orders $Q$ units each time the stock reaches zero has the average cost

$$
C(Q) = \frac{KD}{Q} + \frac{hQ}{2} .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

The stock falls from $Q$ to zero in a time $Q / D$, and the pattern then repeats. One such cycle costs
$K$ for its order and $h\,Q^2 / (2D)$ for holding, as in the proof of <Ref to="lem-equal-quantities" />.
Dividing the cost of a cycle by its length $Q / D$ gives $C(Q)$.

</Proof>

The first term is the cost of ordering: $D / Q$ orders per unit time at $K$ each. The second is the cost
of holding a mean stock of $Q / 2$.

<Theorem id="thm-eoq" name="Economic order quantity">

For every $Q > 0$,

$$
C(Q) \ \ge\ \sqrt{2KDh}, \qquad\text{with equality if and only if}\qquad Q = Q^* := \sqrt{\frac{2KD}{h}} .
$$

At $Q^*$ the cost of ordering equals the cost of holding.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The product of the two terms of $C(Q)$ is the constant $KDh / 2$, so twice the product of their square
roots is $\sqrt{2KDh}$. Completing the square,

$$
C(Q) - \sqrt{2KDh} = \left(\sqrt{\frac{KD}{Q}} - \sqrt{\frac{hQ}{2}}\right)^2 \ \ge\ 0 .
$$

The square is zero exactly when $KD / Q = hQ / 2$, that is, when the two costs are equal and
$Q^2 = 2KD / h$.

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:eoq-cost
  maxima:eoq
  maxima:eoq-square
" />

The quantity $Q^*$ is the **economic order quantity**. The first-order condition
$C'(Q) = -KD/Q^2 + h/2 = 0$ gives the same quantity, and $C''(Q) = 2KD/Q^3 > 0$ shows that $C$ is convex
(Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 53–54). With the two lemmas, the theorem bounds the cost of every plan.

<Corollary id="cor-eoq-all-plans" name="Optimality among all plans">

Every plan that meets all demand over $[0, T]$, and that begins and ends with no stock, costs at least
$T\sqrt{2KDh}$ over the interval.

</Corollary>

<Proof>

By <Ref to="lem-zero-stock" /> and <Ref to="lem-equal-quantities" />, the plan costs at least as much as
the plan with the same number $n$ of orders, all equal and placed at zero stock. That plan orders
$Q = DT / n$ each time and costs $nK + n\,h\,Q^2 / (2D) = T\,C(Q)$, which is at least $T\sqrt{2KDh}$ by
<Ref to="thm-eoq" />.

</Proof>

<Check ref="maxima:eoq-all-plans" />

<WorkedExample id="ex-eoq-shop">

For the Shop, $Q^* = \sqrt{2 \times 20 \times 10 / 1} = 20$ units, ordered every $Q^* / D = 2$ days. The
average cost is $C(Q^*) = \sqrt{2 \times 20 \times 10 \times 1} = 20$ a day, of which 10 is for ordering
and 10 for holding.

</WorkedExample>

<Check ref="maxima:eoq-shop" />

<Corollary id="cor-eoq-sensitivity" name="Sensitivity">

For every $x > 0$,

$$
\frac{C(xQ^*)}{C(Q^*)} = \frac12 \left(x + \frac1x\right) .
$$

</Corollary>

<Proof>

At $Q^*$ each term of $C$ equals $C(Q^*) / 2$. Replacing $Q^*$ by $xQ^*$ divides the first term by $x$
and multiplies the second by $x$.

</Proof>

<WorkedExample id="ex-eoq-sensitivity">

Ordering half or twice the optimal quantity gives $\tfrac12 (2 + \tfrac12) = 1.25$. For the Shop, orders
of 10 or of 40 units cost 25 a day against 20. Orders of 30 units give $\tfrac12 (\tfrac32 + \tfrac23) =
13/12$, a cost of 21.67 a day.

The cost is equally insensitive to errors in the parameters. If the order cost is taken to be twice its
true value, the computed quantity is $\sqrt2\,Q^*$ and the cost is $\tfrac12 (\sqrt2 + 1/\sqrt2) = 1.061$
times the optimal cost, an increase of 6%.

</WorkedExample>

<Check refs="
  maxima:sensitivity
  maxima:sensitivity-shop
" />

<Remark id="rem-eoq-time">

The plan has a mean stock of $Q / 2$ and a flow of $D$. By <Ref to="thm-littles-law-limit" /> a unit
waits $Q / (2D)$ in stock on average, which is 1 day for the Shop at $Q^*$ (Axsäter, 2015, p. 52).

</Remark>

<Check ref="maxima:waiting-time" />

### Lead time

Suppose that, in place of the fourth property of <Ref to="asm-shop" />, an order arrives a **lead time**
$L$ after it is placed.

<Proposition id="prop-reorder-point" name="Reorder point">

Let $L \le Q / D$. The plan that orders $Q$ units each time the stock falls to $r = DL$ has the same
stock at every moment as the plan of <Ref to="prop-eoq-cost" />, and the same average cost.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

During the lead time the demand is $DL$. An order placed when the stock is $DL$ therefore arrives at the
moment the stock reaches zero. Since $L \le Q / D$, the previous order has arrived before the next is
placed.

</Proof>

The level $r$ is the **reorder point** (Snyder and Shen, 2019, p. 56). For the Shop with $L = 1$ day,
$r = 10$ units. When $L > Q / D$, an order is placed while an earlier one is still on its way, and the rule
must count the stock on order. Chapter 6 does so.

<Check ref="maxima:reorder-point" />

### Periodic review

A station that inspects its stock only at intervals cannot order at the exact moment the stock reaches a
given level.

<Definition id="def-s-s-policy" name="Periodic review and (s, S) policies">

Under **periodic review** with **review period** $R$, the station inspects its stock at times
$0, R, 2R, \dots$ and can order only then. An **$(s, S)$ policy**, with $s < S$, orders at a review if the
stock is below the **reorder level** $s$, and then orders the quantity that raises the stock to the
**order-up-to level** $S$.

</Definition>

<Proposition id="prop-periodic-review">

Let the lead time be zero. The $(s, S)$ policy with $s = DR$ and $S = s + Q$ meets all demand, and its
average cost is at most $C(Q) + hDR$.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

A review that places no order finds a stock of at least $s = DR$, which lasts until the next review. A
review that places an order raises the stock to $S > s$. Hence the stock is never exhausted between
reviews.

At a review that places an order the stock $y$ satisfies $0 \le y < s$, so the order $S - y$ exceeds
$Q$. The policy therefore places at most $D / Q$ orders per unit time. Between two orders the stock falls
linearly from $S$ to some $y < s$, so its mean is below $(S + s)/2 = Q/2 + DR$.

</Proof>

<WorkedExample id="ex-periodic-review">

With hourly reviews, $R = 1/24$ day, the Shop's reorder level is $s = 10/24 \approx 0.417$ units. With
$Q = Q^* = 20$ the order-up-to level is $S = 20.417$, and the average cost is at most
$20 + 0.417 = 20.42$ a day. With reviews every minute the bound is $20.007$.

</WorkedExample>

<Check ref="maxima:periodic-review" />

<Remark id="rem-random-demand">

When demand is random and each order has a fixed cost, an
$(s, S)$ policy is optimal under general conditions, a theorem of Scarf (Snyder and Shen, 2019,
pp. 129–136). The reorder level must then also protect against the variation in demand, which chapters 6
and 7 treat.

</Remark>

### The same trade-off on a line

<WorkedExample id="ex-dispatch-interval" name="The interval between trips">

A train collects from a mine that produces $\lambda = 36$ units a day. Each round trip costs $F = 20$, and
each unit waiting at the mine costs $h = 1$ a day. If the train calls every $T$ days and takes all the
stock, the mine's stock rises from zero to $\lambda T$ between calls and has mean $\lambda T / 2$. The
average cost is

$$
\frac{F}{T} + \frac{h \lambda T}{2},
$$

which is $C(\lambda T)$ for an order cost $F$ and a demand rate $\lambda$. By <Ref to="thm-eoq" /> the best
interval is $T^* = \sqrt{2F / (h\lambda)} = 1.054$ days, with a load of $\lambda T^* = 37.95$ units and a
cost of 37.95 a day.

A train of capacity $\kappa = 30$ cannot carry that load. The constraint $\lambda T \le \kappa$ is
binding: $C$ is convex, so the least cost on $T \le 5/6$ is at $T = 5/6$ day, where it is
$24 + 15 = 39$ a day. By <Ref to="cor-eoq-sensitivity" />, with $x = 30 / 37.95$, the constraint raises
the cost by 2.8%.

</WorkedExample>

<Check ref="maxima:dispatch-interval" />

## In the simulator

The `classic.reorder` template is the Shop of <Ref to="asm-shop" />: demand of 10 units a day, an order
cost of 20, a holding cost of 1 per unit per day and no lead time, run for 20 days. The station reviews
its stock every hour.

<Scenario template="classic.reorder" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.reorder {}
```

A policy for a station that orders has a `review` table with a target, as a policy for a train has a
target for each stop. At each review the policy orders the difference between the target and the stock,
when the difference is positive. `ops.min_max { min = s, max = S }` is the $(s, S)$ policy of
<Ref to="def-s-s-policy" />, with amounts in thousandths of a unit. The template's reference policy is that of
<Ref to="ex-periodic-review" />:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.reorder
return ops.policy {
  review = { target = ops.min_max { min = 417, max = 20417 } },
}
```

Run it, then run copies with `max = 10417` and `max = 40417`, which order 10 and 40 units at a time. The
metric **Total cost**, divided by the 20 days of the run, is the average cost.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Whole orders in a finite run.** The average cost is a long-run limit. A run of 20 days places a whole
  number of orders, so a quantity that does not divide the total demand costs more than $C(Q)$. Orders of
  30 units make 7 orders for 200 units of demand, where the model averages $6\tfrac23$.
- **Periodic review.** The station can order only at a review, as in <Ref to="def-s-s-policy" />. With the
  template's hourly reviews the cost lies between $C(Q)$ and the bound of
  <Ref to="prop-periodic-review" />.
- **Timing of costs.** The simulator charges the order cost when an order is placed, and the holding cost
  each minute on the stock present.

## Exercises

Each exercise uses the values in the table under [The Shop](#the-shop), except where it states
otherwise.

1. Let the order cost be $K = 40$. Compute the economic order quantity and its average cost.

<Answer>

$Q^* = \sqrt{2 \times 40 \times 10} = 20\sqrt2 \approx 28.28$ units, and
$C(Q^*) = \sqrt{2 \times 40 \times 10} \approx 28.28$ a day. Doubling $K$ multiplies both by $\sqrt2$.

<Check ref="maxima:dearer-orders" />

</Answer>

2. Show that if the demand rate is multiplied by 4, the economic order quantity doubles. State what
   happens to the time between orders.

<Answer>

$Q^*$ is proportional to $\sqrt D$, so it doubles. The time between orders is
$Q^* / D = \sqrt{2K / (hD)}$, which halves.

<Check ref="maxima:square-root-law" />

</Answer>

3. Find the range of order quantities whose average cost is within 2% of the optimal cost.

<Answer>

By <Ref to="cor-eoq-sensitivity" /> the condition is $\tfrac12 (x + 1/x) \le 1.02$, that is,
$x^2 - 2.04x + 1 \le 0$. The roots are $x = 0.819$ and $x = 1.221$, so any quantity from 16.4 to 24.4
units is within 2% of the optimal cost.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-two-percent" />

</Answer>

4. Run the reference policy with `max = 30417`, which orders 30 units at a time. Compare the cost a day
   with $C(30) = 21.67$, and account for the difference.

<Answer>

The run costs about 22.8 a day. In 20 days it uses 200 units and places 7 orders of 30, where the model
averages $6\tfrac23$ orders. It pays for a seventh order and has part of it in stock at the end of the
run.

<Check ref="test:on classic.reorder with hourly reviews, ordering 30 at a time costs about 22.8 a day against the model's 21.67, because a 20-day run places 7 orders" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

Each stop costs a train time, and each unit moved adds to it, which corresponds to the fixed cost and the
cost per unit of an order. A policy that tops a station up by a few units at every visit incurs the stop
time often. One that delivers a full load at longer intervals incurs it less often, and the
station's mean stock is higher.

</GameNote>
