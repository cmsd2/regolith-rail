## What you will learn

- To balance the fixed cost of ordering against the cost of holding stock, and derive
  the economic order quantity.
- To see why the cost barely changes near the best quantity, and what that means for
  rounding and estimation errors.
- To combine an order quantity with a reorder point in an (s, S) policy, and run it.

## The problem

Part I built a line and measured it. Part II turns to the decisions inside one station:
how much to order, when, and how much to keep in reserve. It starts with the oldest of
them. Many orders cost something whatever their size: a delivery, a set-up, paperwork.
Ordering little and often wastes those fixed costs, while ordering a lot at once fills
the shelves with stock that costs money to hold.

The economic order quantity answers how much to order when demand is steady. It is one
of the oldest results in operations research, and its reasoning, trading a cost that
falls with the quantity against one that rises, appears in many other decisions
(Axsäter, 2015, pp. 45–48; Taha, 2017, pp. 507–510).

## The model

### The economic order quantity

Let demand be a steady $D$ units a day, let each order cost $K$, and let each unit held
cost $h$ a day. Order $Q$ units whenever stock runs out. There are $D/Q$ orders a day,
and stock falls steadily from $Q$ to zero, so on average half an order is held. The cost
a day is

$$
C(Q) = \frac{KD}{Q} + \frac{hQ}{2}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:eoq-cost" />

The first term falls as $Q$ grows and the second rises. Setting the derivative to zero
gives the economic order quantity and its cost:

$$
Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2KD}{h}}, \qquad C(Q^*) = \sqrt{2KDh}.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:eoq" />

At $Q^*$ the two terms are equal: the best quantity spends as much on ordering as on
holding.

<Check ref="maxima:eoq-terms-balance" />

With $D = 10$, $K = 20$ and $h = 1$, the order is 20 units, placed every two days, and
the cost is 20 a day.

<Check ref="maxima:eoq-defaults" />

### A flat bottom

Order $x$ times the best quantity and the cost is

$$
\frac{C(xQ^*)}{C(Q^*)} = \frac{1}{2}\left(x + \frac{1}{x}\right)
$$

times the best. Ordering half again as much costs only about 8.3% more, and ordering
double or half as much costs 25% more. An estimate of $D$ or $K$ that is somewhat wrong,
or a quantity rounded to a convenient size, costs little.

<Check ref="maxima:flat-cost" />

For the worked case, ordering 10 or 40 at a time costs 25 a day, and ordering 30 costs
about 21.67.

<Check ref="maxima:order-sizes" />

### When to order: (s, S)

The quantity says how much. A station that can only order at a review, on a schedule,
also needs a reorder point $s$: order when stock falls below the demand expected before
the next review, and order up to $S = s + Q^*$. This is an (s, S) policy (Axsäter, 2015,
pp. 115–116).

With 10 units a day, no lead time and hourly reviews, the next hour's demand is
$10/24$ of a unit, 417 thousandths rounded up. The policy orders up to $417 + 20{,}000 =
20{,}417$ thousandths whenever stock falls below 417.

<Check ref="maxima:reorder-point" />

When orders take time to arrive, the reorder point must cover that time too; chapter 6
works it out as the protection interval. When demand is random, it also carries safety
stock, which chapter 7 sets.

## In the simulator

The `classic.reorder` template, with its default steady demand, is this model. Demand is
10 units a day, orders cost 20 and arrive immediately, and holding costs 1 a unit a day.

<Scenario template="classic.reorder" />

```lua runnable script
return classic.reorder {}
```

The reference policy is the (s, S) policy above:

```lua runnable scenario=classic.reorder
return ops.policy {
  review = { target = ops.min_max { min = 417, max = 20417 } },
}
```

A test reviews every minute instead of every hour, so the reorder point is 7 thousandths,
and checks that the simulated cost is within one cost unit of 20 a day, with no unmet
demand.

<Check ref="test:costs the economic order quantity's analytic cost with steady demand and no lead time" />

Now make two copies of the reference policy, one with `max = 10417` and one with
`max = 40417`, and compare all three in **Batch**. Ordering 10 or 40 at a time costs 25 a
day against 20, as the model predicts; the test that checks it reviews every minute, so
that its reorder point is 7 thousandths and the cost is the model's.

<Check ref="test:on steady-demand classic.reorder reviewed every minute, ordering 10 or 40 at a time costs 25 a day, and ordering 20 costs 20, each within one cost unit" />

## Where the simulator differs

- **Whole orders in a finite run.** The model averages over an endless cycle. A run of
  20 days places a whole number of orders, so quantities that don't divide the total
  demand evenly cost a little more than the model says. Ordering 30 at a time places 7
  orders for 200 units of demand, not $6\tfrac{2}{3}$.
- **Reviews, not continuous watching.** Stock can only be ordered at a review. With
  hourly reviews and no lead time, the reorder point covers one hour of demand so that
  stock never runs out between reviews.
- **Order costs are charged when an order is placed**, and holding costs each minute on
  what is held.

## Exercises

1. The order cost doubles to 40. What are the new order quantity and cost a day?

<Answer>

$Q^* = 20\sqrt{2} \approx 28.28$, and the cost rises by the same factor of $\sqrt{2}$, to
about 28.28 a day.

<Check ref="maxima:dearer-orders" />

</Answer>

2. Demand quadruples. How does the order quantity change?

<Answer>

It doubles. $Q^*$ grows with the square root of demand, so larger operations order
relatively less often per unit.

<Check ref="maxima:square-root-law" />

</Answer>

3. With the defaults, how much more than the best does ordering 10 at a time cost?

<Answer>

25 a day instead of 20, a quarter more: ordering half the best quantity costs
$(1/2 + 2)/2 = 5/4$ times as much.

<Check ref="maxima:flat-cost" />

</Answer>

4. Order 30 at a time with the template's hourly reviews, `max = 30417`. What does it
   cost a day, and why is it more than the model's 21.67?

<Answer>

About 22.8 a day. A run of 20 days uses 200 units and places 7 orders of 30, not the
$6\tfrac{2}{3}$ the model averages over, so it pays for a seventh order and holds part of
it to the end of the run.

<Check ref="test:on classic.reorder with hourly reviews, ordering 30 at a time costs about 22.8 a day against the model's 21.67, because a 20-day run places 7 orders" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

Each stop costs a train time to dwell, and each unit moved adds to it, which plays a
part like the fixed and per-unit costs of an order. A policy that tops a station up by a
few units at every visit spends that time often; one that waits and delivers a full load
spends it less often but leaves the station lower in between.

</GameNote>
