## What you will learn

- To define the rates of flow into and out of a place, and to prove that bounded stock makes them equal
  over a long period.
- To prove a bound on the flow a vehicle can carry that holds for every policy, and to measure a line's
  demand against it.
- To state Little's law, $L = \lambda W$, and to prove it by counting.
- To use Little's law to compute how long a unit of stock spends in each part of a line.

## The problem

Chapter 1 compared a line's demand with its capacity and found the steady states of two policies. Each
result concerned a particular policy or a particular cycle. This chapter establishes relations between
rates and stock that hold under every policy.

The first is flow balance: over a long period, as much flows out of a station as flows in. The second
bounds the flow that a vehicle can carry, whatever rule decides its loads. The third is Little's law.
It states that the mean number of units in a system equals the rate at which units arrive multiplied by
the mean time a unit spends there (Little, 1961; Eiselt and Sandblom, 2022, p. 461). The law converts a
stock into a time. The chapter uses it to locate the stock on a line and to distinguish stock that
protects a consumer from stock that serves no one.

## The model

### Rates of flow

<Definition id="def-flow-rates" name="Rates of flow">

Consider a place that stores stock, such as a station or a vehicle, over an interval $[0, T]$. Let $I(T)$
be the total amount that enters the place during the interval and $O(T)$ the total amount that leaves.
The average **rates of flow** in and out are $\lambda_{\text{in}} = I(T)/T$ and
$\lambda_{\text{out}} = O(T)/T$.

</Definition>

At a station, stock enters by production or unloading and leaves by consumption or loading. On a vehicle,
stock enters by loading and leaves by unloading.

<Proposition id="prop-flow-balance" name="Flow balance">

Let $x(t)$ be the stock of a place with capacity $\bar x$. Then

$$
x(T) = x(0) + I(T) - O(T), \qquad\text{and so}\qquad
\big|\lambda_{\text{in}} - \lambda_{\text{out}}\big| \le \frac{\bar x}{T} .
$$

If the two rates have limits as $T \to \infty$, the limits are equal.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

The first equation is the account of the stock: what is present at time $T$ is what was present at time
0, with what entered added and what left removed. Both $x(0)$ and $x(T)$ lie between 0 and $\bar x$, so
$|I(T) - O(T)| = |x(T) - x(0)| \le \bar x$. Dividing by $T$ gives the inequality, and its right-hand side
tends to zero.

</Proof>

<Check ref="maxima:flow-balance" />

For a station with $\bar x = 30$, the two rates differ by at most 3 units a day over 10 days and by at most
0.3 over 100 days.

<Corollary id="cor-throughput" name="Throughput of a line">

On the line of <Ref to="asm-line" />, suppose the train loads only at the mine and unloads only at the
dome, and suppose the long-run rates exist. Then four rates are equal: the mine's production, the train's
loading, the train's unloading and the dome's consumption. Their common value $\lambda$, the
**throughput** of the line, satisfies $\lambda \le \min(u, v)$.

</Corollary>

<Proof>

Apply <Ref to="prop-flow-balance" /> to three places in turn. At the mine, production equals loading. On
the train, whose stock is its cargo and whose capacity is $\kappa$, loading equals unloading. At the dome,
unloading equals consumption. The mine produces at a rate of at most $v$ and the dome consumes at a rate
of at most $u$.

</Proof>

The corollary proves the statement about the surplus in <Ref to="rem-every-policy" />. With $v = 45$ and $u = 36$ units a day, the throughput
is at most 36, so the mine loses at least 9 units a day of production under every policy. A policy
determines where the stock is and when a station runs short. It cannot raise the long-run flow above what
the consumer takes.

### Capacity and utilisation

<Ref to="prop-capacity" /> bounds the throughput of a cycle that repeats one load. The same bound holds
for any sequence of loads.

<Proposition id="prop-capacity-bound" name="Capacity bound">

Suppose the train starts empty at the mine, and suppose it loads only at the mine and unloads only at
the dome. Let $D_n$ be the total amount it unloads in its first $n$ round trips, and let $H_n$ be the
duration of those round trips. Then $D_n / H_n \le f(\kappa)$, whatever the policy.

</Proposition>

<Proof>

Let $\ell_i$ and $\delta_i$ be the amounts loaded and unloaded in round trip $i$. Each $\delta_i$ is at
most the cargo aboard, which is at most $\kappa$, so $D_n \le n\kappa$. The train cannot unload more than
it has loaded, so $\sum_i \ell_i \ge D_n$. By <Ref to="asm-line" />, round trip $i$ lasts
$2\tau + (t_0 + t_1 \ell_i) + (t_0 + t_1 \delta_i)$. Summing over the $n$ round trips and using
$n \ge D_n / \kappa$,

$$
H_n = 2n(\tau + t_0) + t_1 \Big(\sum_i \ell_i + \sum_i \delta_i\Big)
\ \ge\ \frac{D_n}{\kappa}\, 2(\tau + t_0) + 2 t_1 D_n = \frac{D_n\, T(\kappa)}{\kappa} .
$$

Hence $D_n / H_n \le \kappa / T(\kappa) = f(\kappa)$.

</Proof>

<Definition id="def-utilisation" name="Utilisation">

The **utilisation** of the line is the ratio of the demand to the capacity, $\rho = u / f(\kappa)$.

</Definition>

<Corollary id="cor-overload" name="Overload">

If $\rho > 1$, then under every policy of <Ref to="prop-capacity-bound" /> the demand left unmet during
the first $n$ round trips is at least $(u - f(\kappa))\,H_n - \bar x$.

</Corollary>

<Proof>

The dome can consume only its initial stock, at most $\bar x$, and what the train delivers, which is
$D_n \le f(\kappa)\,H_n$. The demand during the round trips is $u\,H_n$, and the unmet demand is the
demand less the consumption.

</Proof>

<WorkedExample id="ex-utilisation">

For the line of <Ref to="asm-line" />, $f(\kappa) = 540/7$ units a day by <Ref to="ex-capacity" />, and

$$
\rho = \frac{36}{540/7} = \frac{7}{15} \approx 0.47 .
$$

</WorkedExample>

<Check refs="
  maxima:capacity-bound-proof
  maxima:capacity-utilisation
" />

<Remark id="rem-utilisation">

The condition $\rho \le 1$ is necessary for a policy to meet demand, by <Ref to="cor-overload" />. It is
not sufficient: by <Ref to="ex-half-capacity" />, balancing fails to meet demand on a line with
$\rho = 0.47$.

</Remark>

### Little's law

Consider any system that units enter and later leave. The system may be a single station or vehicle, or
a whole line.

<Definition id="def-system-averages" name="Averages of a system">

Let units $i = 1, \dots, n$ arrive at a system at times $a_i$ and depart at times $d_i \ge a_i$, all
within $[0, T]$. Unit $i$ spends the time $W_i = d_i - a_i$ in the system. Let $N(t)$ be the number of
units present at time $t$, that is, the number of $i$ with $a_i \le t < d_i$. The **mean number present**
$L_T$, the **arrival rate** $\lambda_T$ and the **mean time in the system** $W_T$ are

$$
L_T = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T N(t)\,dt, \qquad \lambda_T = \frac{n}{T}, \qquad
W_T = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} W_i .
$$

</Definition>

<WorkedExample id="ex-sample-path">

Four units arrive at hours 0, 1, 2 and 5 and depart at hours 3, 4, 6 and 7. Take $T = 8$. The number
present is 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 2, 1, 0 in the eight successive hours, so the integral of $N$ is 12 and
$L_T = 12/8 = 1.5$. The arrival rate is $\lambda_T = 4/8 = 0.5$ an hour. The times in the system are
3, 3, 4 and 2 hours, so $W_T = 12/4 = 3$ hours. These satisfy $1.5 = 0.5 \times 3$.

</WorkedExample>

<Theorem id="thm-littles-law" name="Little's law, finite form">

Under the conditions of <Ref to="def-system-averages" />,

$$
L_T = \lambda_T\, W_T .
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $N(t)$ as a sum over units of the indicator that unit $i$ is present at time $t$. Integrating term
by term, each unit contributes the length of the interval $[a_i, d_i)$:

$$
\int_0^T N(t)\,dt = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \int_0^T \mathbf{1}\{a_i \le t < d_i\}\,dt = \sum_{i=1}^{n} W_i .
$$

Dividing by $T$ gives $L_T = \dfrac{n}{T} \cdot \dfrac{1}{n} \sum_i W_i = \lambda_T\, W_T$.

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:sample-path
  maxima:littles-law
  maxima:littles-law-area
" />

The proof counts the same area in two ways: as the number present, summed over time, and as the time
present, summed over units. The finite form requires every unit to arrive and depart within $[0, T]$, so
that the system is empty at both ends. A system observed over a long period satisfies the law in the
limit without that requirement.

<Theorem id="thm-littles-law-limit" name="Little's law">

Let $A(t)$ be the number of units that arrive in $[0, t]$. Suppose the limits

$$
\lambda = \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{A(t)}{t} \qquad\text{and}\qquad
W = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} W_i
$$

exist and are finite. Then the limit $L = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T N(t)\,dt$ exists, and
$L = \lambda W$.

</Theorem>

<Proof sketch>

A unit that has departed by time $T$ contributes its whole time $W_i$ to the integral of $N$ over
$[0, T]$. A unit that has arrived by time $T$ contributes at most $W_i$. Hence

$$
\sum_{i\,:\,d_i \le T} W_i \ \le\ \int_0^T N(t)\,dt \ \le\ \sum_{i\,:\,a_i \le T} W_i .
$$

Divided by $T$, the right-hand side is $A(T)/T$ multiplied by the mean of the first $A(T)$ times in the
system, and tends to $\lambda W$. The two sides differ by the times of the units present at time $T$. The
existence of $W$ implies $W_n / n \to 0$, and it follows that this difference is small compared with
$T$. Stidham (1974) gives the complete argument.

</Proof>

<Check refs="
  maxima:littles-law-long-run
  maxima:littles-law-sandwich
" />

Little (1961) first proved the law, for systems in a stationary regime. The proof by counting needs no
probability.

<Remark id="rem-little">

The law assumes nothing about the pattern of arrivals or the order in which units leave. It holds under
every policy, and therefore cannot rank policies. The rate $\lambda$ is the rate
at which units enter the system. Where demand is lost or production stops, it is the rate of the units
that get through, which Taha (2017, p. 667) calls the effective arrival rate. The argument applies
unchanged to divisible stock, with units taken arbitrarily small.

</Remark>

### Stock as time

Read as $W = L / \lambda$, Little's law gives the mean time a unit spends in a place from the mean stock
there and the flow through it. Axsäter (2015, p. 52) reads the stock of a store in this way.

<Definition id="def-cover" name="Cover">

At a station that supplies a consumer at rate $\lambda$ and has mean stock $L$, the **cover** is
$L / \lambda$, the time for which the mean stock would meet the demand with no further delivery.

</Definition>

By Little's law the cover is also the mean time a unit waits at the station before it is consumed. On a
line, stock waits in three kinds of place. At the consumer's station the time is cover. Aboard a vehicle
it is travel. At a station that neither produces nor consumes, the time brings a unit no nearer to use.

The next result locates the stock of a line in a case where the total is known. Call the line of
<Ref to="asm-line" /> **balanced** if $v = u$.

<Proposition id="prop-balanced-line" name="Stock on a balanced line">

Let the line be balanced with $v = u = \lambda$, and let it be in the steady state of
<Ref to="prop-drain-fill" /> under drain-and-fill, with round trips of duration $T = T(\bar a)$. Let $S$
be the total stock on the line, at the stations and aboard the train. Then $S$ is constant and a unit
spends the time $S / \lambda$ on the line. The mean stocks are

$$
L_{\text{mine}} = \frac{\lambda T}{2}, \qquad L_{\text{train}} = \frac{\lambda T}{2}, \qquad
L_{\text{dome}} = S - \lambda T .
$$

</Proposition>

<Proof>

In this steady state no production is lost and no demand is unmet, so stock enters and leaves the line at
the same rate $\lambda$ and $S$ is constant. <Ref to="thm-littles-law-limit" /> for the whole line, with
$L = S$, gives $W = S / \lambda$.

The train drains the mine at each arrival. The mine's stock then rises at rate $\lambda$ for a time $T$,
from 0 to $\lambda T$, so its mean is $\lambda T / 2$.

By <Ref to="asm-line" /> a unit is aboard from the train's arrival at the mine until its arrival at the
dome, a time $t_0 + t_1 \bar a + \tau = T / 2$. Units pass through the train at rate $\lambda$, so
<Ref to="thm-littles-law-limit" /> gives a mean cargo of $\lambda T / 2$.

The dome has the remainder, $S - \lambda T$.

</Proof>

<WorkedExample id="ex-balanced-line">

Let $v = u = 3/2$ units an hour, and let the line start with 15 units at each station, so $S = 30$ and a
unit spends $30 / \tfrac32 = 20$ hours on the line. With $T = 500/57$ hours from
<Ref to="ex-needed-load" />,

$$
L_{\text{mine}} = L_{\text{train}} = \frac{125}{19} \approx 6.58, \qquad
L_{\text{dome}} = \frac{320}{19} \approx 16.84 .
$$

A unit spends 4.39 hours at the mine and as long aboard, then 11.23 hours at the dome. If the train's speed
doubles, each leg takes 2 hours and $T = 260/57 \approx 4.56$ hours. The mine and the train then have
$65/19 \approx 3.42$ units each and the dome has $440/19 \approx 23.16$, a cover of 15.44 hours. The unit
still spends 20 hours on the line. The faster train moves stock from the mine and the train to the dome,
where it is cover.

</WorkedExample>

<Check refs="
  maxima:balanced-line-formula
  maxima:balanced-line
  maxima:balanced-line-fast
" />

<WorkedExample id="ex-relay" name="A line with a relay station">

Consider a line with a third station, a junction between the mine and the dome that neither produces nor
consumes. Each of its two legs takes 2 hours. The mine produces 45 units a day and the dome consumes 34
units a day, so $u = 17/12$ units an hour. The other parameters are those of <Ref to="asm-line" />. Suppose the train stops at
the junction in each direction and moves nothing there. A round trip then has four legs and four stops,
two of which move the load $a$, and lasts $4 \times 2 + 4 t_0 + 2 t_1 a$ hours. As in
<Ref to="prop-needed-load" />, the load that meets demand solves $a = u\,(8 + 4 t_0 + 2 t_1 a)$. Under
drain-and-fill the dome's station is full after each stop and falls by that load before the next. Hence

$$
\bar a = \frac{4420}{343} \approx 12.89\ \text{units}, \qquad
L_{\text{dome}} = \bar x - \frac{\bar a}{2} = \frac{8080}{343} \approx 23.56\ \text{units},
$$

a cover of 16.63 hours.

</WorkedExample>

## In the simulator

The run view's **Metrics** include a table of [long-run averages](/docs/metrics#long-run-averages). For
each station it reports the mean stock $L$ and the flows in and out, in units a day. The column
**Stock ÷ out** is $L / \lambda$. The column **Waited** is the mean time in the station measured
directly, by following each unit from arrival to departure, first in, first out. The line's row adds the
cargo aboard to the stock at the stations.

The `relay` starter is the line of <Ref to="ex-relay" />, with a consumption rate that varies. It opens
with balancing, which the case study examines. Drain-and-fill extends to it with a third role. The
`"relay"` role marks a station that passes stock on, and `ops.pass_through {}` sets no target for it, so
the train moves nothing there:

<Scenario starter="relay" />

```lua runnable scenario=relay fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual {
    Mine = "supply",
    Junction = "relay",
    Dome = "demand",
  },
  target = {
    supply = ops.drain {},
    relay = ops.pass_through {},
    demand = ops.fill {},
  },
}
```

Set the dome's `variability = 0` in the scenario script and run. The junction stays empty. The run starts
with 15 units at the dome, below the steady state's range, so the mean over a short run is below the
model's value and rises with the run's length.

## Case study: dead stock

On a line with three stations that store a resource, balancing sets every site's target to the mean stock
of the three. The junction of the relay line has no producer and no consumer, so its stock changes only
when the train stops there, and at each stop the train moves it towards the current mean. The mine's
station is usually close to full and the dome's close to empty, so the mean, and with it the junction's
stock, stays near 13 units. Stock kept at a station that never uses it, and that the train seldom moves
on, is **dead stock**.

<WorkedExample id="ex-dead-stock">

A 10-day run of the `relay` starter under `balance-stock`, with the dome's `variability = 0`, gives a mean
stock of 13.29 units at the junction and 6.27 at the dome. The stations have 44.11 units in all and the
train carries 7.25, a total of 51.36, and the line delivers 32.86 units a day. By
<Ref to="thm-littles-law-limit" /> a unit spends

$$
\frac{51.36}{32.86} \times 24 \approx 37.5\ \text{hours}
$$

on the line, of which $13.29 / 32.86 \times 24 \approx 9.7$ hours are at the junction. The dome's cover is
4.6 hours, and the dome runs short. Under drain-and-fill with the junction as a relay, the line has more
stock, 64.12 units, and a unit spends 45.3 hours on it. None of that time is at the junction, and the
dome's cover is 16.2 hours.

</WorkedExample>

<Check refs="
  maxima:relay-reading
  test:on relay with steady demand, balancing keeps 13.29 units at the junction, where a unit waits 62.9 hours, and 6.27 at the dome, where a unit waits 4.53 hours; the line has 51.36 units in stock and delivers 32.86 a day, and the dome goes short
" />

The comparison shows that the total stock on a line does not measure how well the line serves its
consumer. The drain-and-fill run has a quarter more stock than the balancing run and more than three
times the cover, because none of its stock waits at the junction.

The **Waited** column tests Little's law against a direct measurement. At the dome a unit waits 4.53
hours, and $\lambda W = 32.86 \times 4.53 / 24 = 6.20$ units, within 2% of the mean stock of 6.27. At the
junction a unit waits 62.9 hours and only 4.11 units a day leave. The units still present when the run
ends are excluded from the measured waits, and over 10 days $\lambda W$ is 81% of the junction's mean
stock. The finite form of the law does not apply to a run that ends with units present, and the long-run
form is approached slowly where units turn over slowly.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Finite runs.** <Ref to="thm-littles-law-limit" /> concerns long-run averages. A run starts from a
  given stock and ends after a set number of days, so its averages include the initial state and exclude
  the waits in progress at the end.
- **Loading on arrival.** The train loads or unloads when it arrives and then waits for the stop's
  duration, as <Ref to="asm-line" /> states. The time aboard in <Ref to="prop-balanced-line" /> depends on
  this.
- **Order of departure.** The **Waited** column follows units first in, first out. Little's law needs no
  order, so **Stock ÷ out** is the same for any order.
- **Cargo as stock.** The line's row includes the cargo aboard, so a policy that keeps the train loaded
  keeps that stock, and the corresponding time, in transit.

<GameNote>

In the rail game, a line often passes through a station with nothing attached to it, such as a junction
or a station whose buildings have been removed, and trains balance stock into it as into any other. On a
long line, the stock at such a station is stock that the domes at the ends are waiting for. The mean stock
of an idle station, divided by the line's flow, is the number of hours that each unit spends there to no
purpose.

<Evidence level="observed" />

</GameNote>
