## What you will learn

- To describe an operation by its flows: the rates at which stock enters and leaves each
  place, and why steady stock means the rates balance.
- To read a vehicle's capacity as a bound on the flow it can carry, and to check a line
  against that bound before any policy is written.
- To use Little's law, stock equals flow times time, to turn the stock a place holds into
  the time each unit spends there, and to tell stock that protects from stock that only
  waits.

## The problem

Chapter 1 wrote down a line and asked whether one train could serve it. The answer came
from counting: units a day made, units a day used, trips a day the train can make. That
kind of counting is the first tool of operations research, and it works before anything
about randomness or policy has been decided. A flow is a rate: units a day into a place,
units a day out of it. Stock is what the flows leave behind.

Flows and stock are tied together by one relation that holds for any system in which
things enter, wait and leave: the average stock is the average flow multiplied by the
average time each unit spends inside. It is Little's law, and it is exact under
conditions so mild that it applies to a warehouse, a queue at a counter, a railway line
and a hospital ward (Little, 1961; Taha, 2017, pp. 667–668). This chapter uses it to
read a stock level as a length of time, and to tell stock that protects a station from
stock that merely waits.

## The model

### Rates in and out

Chapter 1's conservation equation counted what a station's stock gained and lost over a
stretch of time. Divide it by the length of that stretch and it becomes a statement
about rates: the stock changes at the rate things are produced and unloaded there, less
the rate they are consumed and loaded. When stock is steady over a long enough period,
the change is zero and the rates in equal the rates out.

$$
\frac{s(t_2) - s(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1} = \text{production} - \text{consumption} - \text{loading} + \text{unloading}.
$$

At a station that only consumes, steady stock means deliveries arrive, on average, at the
rate the station uses them. That sounds obvious, and it is, but it has a sharp edge: a
demand station whose stock is steady is being supplied at exactly its consumption rate,
whatever the policy is doing at each stop. Policies change how much stock the station
holds and how often it runs dry; they cannot change the long-run flow, which the
consumers set. On the two-station line the mine makes 9 units a day more than the dome
uses, and no policy can carry the surplus anywhere, so the mine stalls at that rate
whatever the train does.

### Capacity as a bound on flow

A vehicle carries a load per trip, and makes so many trips a day, so it can carry at
most their product. On the two-station line a full 30-unit load with full stops at both
ends makes a round trip in $28/3$ hours, and an empty run with the shortest stops makes
one in $25/3$ hours. The flow the train can carry between the stations therefore lies
between

$$
30 \times \frac{24}{28/3} = \frac{540}{7} \approx 77.1
\quad\text{and}\quad
30 \times \frac{24}{25/3} = \frac{432}{5} = 86.4 \text{ units a day}.
$$

Either way it is more than twice the 36 units a day the dome uses. The bound is the first
thing to check about any line: if the flow it must carry lies above the bound, no policy
can save it, and if the flow lies well below, the question is not capacity but where the
stock sits.

<Check ref="maxima:flow-bound" />

### Stock as time: Little's law

Let $L$ be the average stock held in some part of a system, $\lambda$ the average flow
through it, and $W$ the average time a unit spends inside. Then

$$
L = \lambda W.
$$

The units make it plausible: stock is units, flow is units a day, so time is days. The
proof is a counting argument over a long period, and it needs no assumption about how
arrivals or departures are distributed, only that what enters eventually leaves and the
averages exist. The relation is most useful turned round, $W = L / \lambda$: the stock a
place holds, divided by the flow through it, is how long each unit waits there.

At a station that uses 36 units a day, 30 units of stock is $30/36$ of a day, 20 hours.
That is the time the station could run with no deliveries, its *cover*, and it is the
first way to read a stock level: not as an amount but as a length of time. A full
30-unit station on the relay line, which uses 34 a day, holds about 21.2 hours.

<Check ref="maxima:cover" />

The same reading applies to a whole line. Add up the stock at every station and on every
vehicle, divide by the rate at which the line delivers, and the result is the average time
a unit takes from being produced to being used. Every place that holds stock adds its
share of that time. At the consumer, the time is protection: it is how long the station
can go without a delivery. In transit, it is the trip. At a station that neither makes
nor uses the resource, the time is pure waiting: the unit is no nearer to being used
when it leaves than when it arrived.

## In the simulator

The `relay` starter is a line of three stations: a mine making 45 units a day, a
junction with no producers or consumers, and a dome using 34 a day, sometimes up to 20%
more or less. The legs are 36,000 units long, so each takes the train two hours, and a
round trip travels for eight. It opens with the balancing baseline.

<Check ref="maxima:relay-legs" />

<Scenario starter="relay" />

Run `balance-stock` and watch the junction on the line map. Its station fills within the
first day and stays about a third full for the rest of the run, while the dome runs short.
Over seeds 1 to 20 the junction holds about 13 units on average, three tenths of the stock
at the stations, and the dome goes short on every seed.

<Check ref="test:on relay over seeds 1 to 20 the balancing baseline keeps about 13 of the line's 52 units at the junction, three tenths of the station stock, and the dome goes short on every seed" />

Now give each station its role. The mine is drained, the dome is filled, and the
junction is passed through: the train neither leaves stock there nor takes any.

```lua runnable scenario=relay fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual {
    Mine = "supply",
    Junction = "relay",
    Dome = "demand",
  },
  target = {
    supply = ops.drain {},
    relay = ops.pass_through {},
    demand = ops.fill {},
  },
}
```

Run it and the junction stays empty, the dome meets all its demand on every one of seeds
1 to 20, and the dome's station holds about 23 units on average, 16 hours of use.

<Check ref="test:on relay over seeds 1 to 20 giving the junction the relay role holds nothing there, meets all demand, and keeps about 23 units at the dome, 16 hours of use" />

Compare the two in **Batch** over 100 seeds. Unmet demand falls to nothing, and the
[empty distance](/docs/metrics#empty-distance-share) share falls too, since the train no
longer runs back and forth topping up a station that uses nothing.

## Case study: dead stock

Why does the junction fill? Balancing aims every station at the line's average stock, and
the junction is a station, so it is aimed at the average like the others. With three
stations, about a third of the stock on the line ends up at the one place that cannot use
it. The starter's description calls it dead stock: metal that was mined and carried and
then does nothing.

Little's law says what that costs. Over seeds 1 to 20 the balancing baseline delivers
about 32.5 units a day and holds about 52 units on the line, 24.6 at the mine, 13.5 at
the junction, 6.5 at the dome and 7.2 on the train. Each unit therefore spends about 38
hours between being mined and being used, and about 10 of those hours parked at the
junction.

<Check ref="test:on relay over seeds 1 to 20 the balancing baseline delivers about 32.5 units a day, so by Little's law a unit spends about 38 hours on the line, 10 of them at the junction" />

Under the roles policy the line holds more stock, not less: about 64 units, since the
train carries a full load most of the way and the dome's station stays near 23. Each unit
spends about 45 hours on the line. The difference is where the hours are spent. Under
balancing, 10 of 38 hours are at a station that uses nothing and the dome, at 6.5 units,
has under five hours of cover. Under roles, none of the time is wasted and the dome holds
16 hours of cover. Holding more stock is not the problem; holding it where it does no
good is.

Little's law is only an identity, and it says nothing about whether a policy is good. Its
use is in reading a line: it turns the stock chart into a statement about time, and it
shows at once where stock is waiting without protecting anyone. Later chapters set how
much time a demand station should hold. This one only says that a relay should hold
none.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Averages over a finite run.** Little's law is exact for long-run averages. A 10-day
  run starts with 30 units on the line and ends with whatever the policy left, so its
  averages carry a small transient. The figures above are averages over the whole run.
- **Rates vary from hour to hour.** The dome's consumption varies by up to 20% around 34
  a day, so its long-run flow is the average of those rates, and a single day's flow can
  differ.
- **Cargo counts as stock on the line.** The figures include what the train carries. A
  policy that keeps the train full holds a full load's worth of time in transit at all
  times.

## Exercises

1. A depot holds 12 units of a part on average, and 36 units a day pass through it. How
   long does a part wait at the depot on average?

<Answer>

By Little's law, $W = L / \lambda = 12 / 36$ of a day, 8 hours.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-waiting-time" />

</Answer>

2. A train carries 40 units a day across a leg that takes two hours. How much cargo is in
   transit on that leg on average?

<Answer>

$L = \lambda W = 40 \times 2/24 = 10/3$, about 3.33 units. Little's law works for the leg
as it does for a station: the leg is a place that units enter, spend two hours in and
leave.

<Check ref="maxima:exercise-in-transit" />

</Answer>

3. On `relay`, give the mine and the dome their roles but leave the junction with no role,
   so that it takes the `any` role and a `balance` target. How much stock does the junction
   hold now, and does the dome still go short?

<Answer>

About 20 units, more than under the baseline: the mine is drained onto the train, the
dome is filled to its capacity, and the junction is balanced towards the average of a
line that now holds more. The dome meets all its demand, because its own role fills it,
but a third of the line's stock still waits where nothing uses it.

<Check ref="test:on relay over seeds 1 to 20 giving the junction the any role with a balance target parks about 20 units there, while the mine and dome are served by their roles and all demand is met" />

</Answer>

<GameNote>

In the rail game, a line through a station with nothing attached to it, a junction or a
station whose buildings have been removed, is common, and trains balance stock into it
like any other. On a long line, the stock parked at such a station is stock the domes at
the ends of the line are waiting for. Watching how full an idle station stays is the
quickest sign that a line's trains are wasting the time of every unit they carry.

</GameNote>
