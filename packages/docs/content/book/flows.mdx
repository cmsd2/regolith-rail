## What you will learn

- To describe a place by the rates at which units enter and leave it, and to see why bounded stock makes
  those rates agree over a long run.
- To bound the flow a vehicle can carry, and to measure how close a line runs to that bound.
- To state Little's law, $L = \lambda W$, and to follow the counting argument behind it.
- To read stock as time: how long a unit waits in each place on a line, and what that time does for the
  station that uses the unit.

## The problem

Chapter 1 answered its first question by counting units a day: what the mine made and the dome used,
against what the train could carry. This chapter turns that counting into relations between rates and stock that hold
whatever policy runs the line. The main one is Little's law. It says that the average number of units in
a place equals the rate at which they arrive multiplied by the average time each one stays, and it holds
for a queue at a counter, a warehouse, a train or a whole line (Little, 1961; Eiselt and Sandblom, 2022,
p. 461).

Its use here is in reading a line. A stock level on its own says little. Divided by the flow through the
station, it says how long a unit waits there, and that time either protects a consumer from running short
or does nothing for anyone.

## The model

### Rates in and out

Chapter 1's state equation kept an account of a station's stock. Over a stretch of $T$ days the stock
changes by what came in less what went out. Dividing by $T$ turns the account into average rates:

$$
\frac{x(T) - x(0)}{T} = \lambda_{\text{in}} - \lambda_{\text{out}},
$$

where $\lambda_{\text{in}}$ is the average rate at which units were produced or unloaded there, and
$\lambda_{\text{out}}$ the average rate at which they were used or loaded. A station's stock stays between
0 and its capacity $\bar x$, so the left side is at most $\bar x / T$ in size: for a 30-unit station, 3 a
day over 10 days and 0.3 a day over 100. Over a long run the rates in and out of every station agree. This
is **flow balance**.

<Check ref="maxima:flow-balance" />

A consumer whose demand is met receives, over a long run, what it uses. A policy decides how much stock
sits at the consumer and when it runs short, and the consumer sets the flow. On the two-station line the
mine makes 9 units a day more than the dome uses. Flow balance at the mine means its production has to
stop for that 9 a day on average, as chapter 1 found.

### Capacity and utilisation

The most a vehicle can carry in a day is its capacity multiplied by the trips it can make. Taking the
slowest round trip, which moves a full load at both stops, gives a flow the vehicle can always reach,
$\lambda_{\max}$. The **utilisation** $\rho$ is the share of that flow the demand $\lambda$ uses. For the
two-station line, with chapter 1's slowest round trip,

$$
\lambda_{\max} = \frac{24\,\kappa}{T_{\max}} = \frac{540}{7} \approx 77.1\ \text{units a day}, \qquad
\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{\max}} = \frac{36}{540/7} = \frac{7}{15} \approx 0.47.
$$

<Check ref="maxima:capacity-utilisation" />

A line with a utilisation above 1 cannot keep up under any policy. Below 1 it can, and chapter 1 showed
that a policy can still fail to use the room it has. The question then is where the stock sits.

### Little's law

Take any place that units enter and later leave, such as a station or a train. Start with four
units that arrive at hours 0, 1, 2 and 5 and leave at hours 3, 4, 6 and 7. Count $N(t)$, the units present
at time $t$, hour by hour over the 8 hours, and add up the area under it. Each unit adds one for every hour
it is present, so the area is the total time the units spend inside, $3 + 3 + 4 + 2 = 12$ unit-hours. On
average $L = 12/8 = 1.5$ units are present, units arrive at $\lambda = 4/8 = 0.5$ an hour, and each stays
$W = 12/4 = 3$ hours. And $1.5 = 0.5 \times 3$.

The same count works for any record. Let $A(T)$ be the number of units that arrive by time $T$ and $W_i$
the time unit $i$ stays. If the place is empty at the start and the end,

$$
\int_0^T N(t)\,dt = \sum_{i=1}^{A(T)} W_i,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T N(t)\,dt}_{L} =
\underbrace{\frac{A(T)}{T}}_{\lambda} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{A(T)} \sum_{i=1}^{A(T)} W_i}_{W}.
$$

The left side is the average number present, $L$. The first factor on the right is the arrival rate,
$\lambda$, and the second the average time a unit stays, $W$. So $L = \lambda W$.

<Check ref="maxima:littles-law" />

This is **Little's law**. The argument assumes nothing about how units arrive or in what order they leave.
Over a long run the units present at the start and the end matter less and less, and the law holds for
the long-run averages whenever they exist (Little, 1961). The rate is the rate at which units actually
get in: where some demand is lost or some production stops, it is what gets through, which Taha (2017,
p. 667) calls the effective arrival rate.

### Reading stock as time

Turned round, $W = L / \lambda$: the stock in a place divided by the flow through it is how long a unit
stays. Axsäter (2015, p. 52) reads a store's stock this way, as the average time a unit waits to be used.
On a line there are three kinds of place a unit can wait, and the time means something different in each:

- at a consumer, the time is **cover**, how long the consumer could go on without a delivery;
- aboard a vehicle, the time is the trip;
- at a station that neither makes nor uses the resource, the unit waits and comes no nearer to being used.

Take the two-station line with one change: the mine makes 36 units a day, what the dome uses, and demand
is steady. Under the drain-and-fill rule no production stops and no demand goes unmet, so the line keeps
the 30 units it starts with, 15 at each station. Little's law for the whole line gives each unit
$W = 30 / \lambda = 20$ hours from being mined to being used, with $\lambda = 3/2$ units an hour. The
policy and the train decide where those 20 hours are spent.

- **The mine.** Its stock rises from zero at $\lambda$ after each visit and the train drains it every round
  trip $T$, so it averages $\lambda T / 2$.
- **The train.** A unit is aboard from loading at the mine to unloading on arrival at the dome, which is
  the mine stop and one leg, $t_0 + t_1 \bar a + \tau = T/2$. So it carries $\lambda T / 2$ on average.
- **The dome.** It has the rest, $30 - \lambda T$.

With chapter 1's round trip $T = 500/57 \approx 8.77$ hours,

$$
L_{\text{mine}} = L_{\text{train}} = \frac{\lambda T}{2} = \frac{125}{19} \approx 6.58, \qquad
L_{\text{dome}} = 30 - \lambda T = \frac{320}{19} \approx 16.84.
$$

A unit spends $T/2 \approx 4.39$ hours at the mine, as long again aboard, and $640/57 \approx 11.23$ hours at
the dome, 20 hours in all.

<Check ref="maxima:balanced-line" />

A train at speed 10 halves each leg. The round trip falls to $260/57 \approx 4.56$ hours, the mine and the
train each average $65/19 \approx 3.42$ units, and the dome has $440/19 \approx 23.16$, which lasts
$15.44$ hours. The line still keeps each unit 20 hours. A faster train moves stock off the train and the
mine's station onto the dome's, where it covers the dome's demand.

<Check ref="maxima:balanced-line-fast" />

## In the simulator

The run view's **Metrics** shows a table of
[long-run averages](/docs/metrics#long-run-averages). For each station it gives the average stock $L$ and
the flows in and out a day. Two more columns give times. **Stock ÷ out** is $L / \lambda$, the law's reading of the stock.
**Waited** is $W$ measured without the law, by following each unit from its arrival to its departure,
first in, first out. The line's row adds the cargo aboard the train to the stock at the stations, so the
cargo is the line's stock less the stations'.

The `relay` starter has all three kinds of place. Its line runs from a mine making 45 units a day, through
a junction with no producers or consumers, to a dome using 34 a day. Each leg takes two hours. The starter
opens with balancing, and the case study below reads what it does. The drain-and-fill rule extends to the
junction with a third role:

<Scenario starter="relay" />

```lua runnable scenario=relay fix
return ops.policy {
  classify = ops.roles.manual {
    Mine = "supply",
    Junction = "relay",
    Dome = "demand",
  },
  target = {
    supply = ops.drain {},
    relay = ops.pass_through {},
    demand = ops.fill {},
  },
}
```

The `"relay"` role marks a station that passes stock on, and `ops.pass_through {}` sets no target for it,
so the train neither leaves stock at the junction nor takes any. With steady demand the dome is filled to
30 at every stop and uses the load $\bar a$ before the next, so it averages $30 - \bar a / 2$. A round trip
here has four legs and four stops, two of which move the load, so $\bar a = uT$ with
$T = 4 \times 2 + 4 t_0 + 2 t_1 \bar a$ and $u = 34/24$ an hour. That gives $\bar a = 4420/343 \approx 12.89$
units and a dome averaging $8080/343 \approx 23.56$, which covers $16.63$ hours.

<Check ref="maxima:relay-roles" />

Set the dome's variability to 0 and run it. The junction stays empty. The dome averages 23.00 units over
10 days, 16.24 hours of use: it starts at 15 units, below its steady range, which pulls a short run's
average down. Over 20 days it averages 23.28.

## Case study: dead stock

Under balancing the train aims every site at the average stock of the stations that store metal. The
junction stores metal, so it is one of the three. Nothing is used at the junction, so nothing drains it
between visits, and each time the train passes it moves the junction's stock towards the average of the
three. With the mine's station nearly full and the dome's nearly empty, that average sits well above zero.
Stock that is held where nothing uses it, and that seldom moves on, is **dead stock**.

With the relay line's demand steady, the 10-day run under balancing shows the size of it. The junction
averages 13.29 units and the dome 6.27. The stations hold 44.11 units and the train 7.25, 51.36 in all, and
the line delivers 32.86 a day, so by Little's law a unit spends

$$
\frac{51.36}{32.86} \times 24 \approx 37.5\ \text{hours}
$$

on the line. Of those, $13.29 / 32.86 \times 24 \approx 9.7$ hours are at the junction, while the dome's
stock covers $4.6$ hours, and the dome runs short. Under the drain-and-fill rule, with the junction as a
relay, the line holds more, 64.12 units, and a unit spends $45.3$ hours on it. None of those hours is at the junction, and the dome's stock covers
$16.2$ hours.

<Check refs="
  maxima:relay-reading
  test:on relay with steady demand, balancing keeps 13.29 units at the junction, where a unit waits 62.9 hours, and 6.27 at the dome, where a unit waits 4.53 hours; the line holds 51.36 units and delivers 32.86 a day, and the dome goes short
" />

The table's **Waited** column checks the law against a direct measurement. At the dome a unit waits 4.53
hours, and $\lambda W = 32.86 \times 4.53 / 24 = 6.20$, within 2% of the 6.27 units there. At the junction
a unit waits 62.9 hours, but only 4.11 units a day leave it, and the units still there when the run ends
are left out of the measured waits. Over 10 days $\lambda W$ comes to 81% of the junction's stock. A place
that turns over slowly needs a long run before its averages settle.

## Where the simulator differs

- **Finite runs.** Little's law holds for long-run averages. A run starts from given stock and stops after
  a set number of days, so its averages include the start and leave out the waits still under way at the
  end.
- **Units move at the start of a stop.** The train loads or unloads when it arrives, then waits out the
  stop's time before it leaves. The model of the time aboard uses this.
- **Waited follows units in order.** The measured wait takes units first in, first out. Little's law needs
  no order, so the stock ÷ out column is the same whatever order the station uses.
- **Cargo is stock on the line.** The line's row counts what the train carries, so a policy that keeps the
  train loaded keeps that much stock, and time, in transit.

<GameNote>

In the rail game, a line often passes through a station with nothing attached to it, a junction or a
station whose buildings have been removed, and trains balance stock into it like any other. On a long
line, the stock parked there is stock the domes at the ends are waiting for. How full an idle station
stays, read against the line's flow, says how many hours of it are doing nothing.
