## The problem

Beer passes from a brewery through a distributor and a wholesaler to a retailer. Each
stage orders from the stage before it and waits for shipments, and only the retailer
sees customers. Customer demand hardly changes, yet orders swing more and more the
further upstream they go. This is the bullwhip effect, made famous by the beer game
(Snyder and Shen, 2019, pp. 539–562).

## The scenario

Stations `Stage1` to `StageN` store `Beer` with unlimited room.

- `Stage1` orders from an outside brewery; every later stage orders from the stage
  before it. Each shipment takes `lead_time`.
- Customers buy from the last stage each period, from a discrete distribution. Unmet
  demand is backordered at `backorder_cost` per unit per day.
- Every stage holds stock at `holding_cost` per unit per day and is reviewed once a
  period, a minute in.
- A stage ships what it can when an order arrives and the rest as its own stock
  arrives.

```lua runnable script
return classic.serial_chain {}
```

The defaults are four stages, a two-day lead time, daily reviews, customer demand of
2, 4 or 6 with weights 1, 2 and 1, and 12 units at every stage to start.

## The reference policy

Each stage uses a moving-average order-up-to policy, a common rule and the one that
shows the effect most plainly:

1. Estimate the demand since the last review from the fall in the inventory position.
2. Average the last four estimates.
3. Order up to that average times the periods an order must cover: the lead time plus
   one review period.

The inventory position is stock plus orders on the way, less backorders and less the
orders from the next stage still waiting for stock. Counting what is owed downstream
needs the `line` information level.

```lua
-- In the workbench, picking the template loads the full policy.
local position = stock + on_order - backorders - owed_downstream
local demand = previous_position - position
local target = average(last_four_demands) * (lead_time / review_period + 1)
```

## The reference result

There is no closed form for costs here. Instead, tests check two things over the
defaults:

- **Conservation.** At every event, the beer in stock and in transit equals the starting
  stock plus what the brewery shipped less what customers bought.
- **Variance grows upstream.** Over 20 seeds, after a ten-day warm-up, the variance of
  each stage's daily orders is larger than that of the stage after it, and the last
  stage's orders vary more than customer demand, whose variance is 2.

The moving average updates its forecast from noisy recent demand, and the order-up-to
target multiplies each change by the periods it covers, so every stage passes on a
louder version of what it saw. A longer window or a shorter lead time damps it; sharing
customer demand with every stage removes the part caused by forecasting (Snyder and
Shen, 2019, pp. 539–562; Axsäter, 2015, pp. 167–168).

## Where the simulator differs

- **Information.** In the beer game, players see only their own stage. The reference
  policy reads orders placed by the next stage to count what it owes, which a real
  stage would know from its own order book.
- **Delays.** Orders reach the supplier at once; only shipping takes time. The beer game
  also delays orders.
- **Capacity.** The brewery never runs short, and stations have unlimited room.
- **Better policies.** In a serial chain, base-stock policies on echelon stock, a
  stage's own stock plus everything downstream and in transit, are optimal (Snyder and
  Shen, 2019, pp. 191–197; Axsäter, 2015, pp. 192–198). Writing one is a good exercise.
